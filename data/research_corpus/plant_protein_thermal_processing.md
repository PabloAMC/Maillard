# **Flavor Chemistry and Thermally Induced Transformations in Plant-Based Protein Matrices: A Comprehensive Kinetic and Compositional Analysis**

## **Introduction to Thermally Induced Molecular Transformations in Plant Proteins**

The formulation, processing, and optimization of plant-based protein matrices represent an extraordinarily complex intersection of protein chemistry, lipid oxidation thermodynamics, and Maillard reaction kinetics. As the global demand for plant-based meat analogues accelerates, the scientific community is increasingly focused on understanding the exact chemical pathways that govern flavor generation, off-flavor retention, and the mitigation of processing-induced toxicants. Plant proteins, primarily soy protein isolate and pea protein isolate, are subjected to intense thermal processing techniques, such as high-moisture extrusion, prolonged baking, and high-pressure homogenization, to achieve the anisotropic fibrous structures characteristic of animal meat. However, the application of high thermal energy in the presence of specific intrinsic and extrinsic precursors—namely reducing sugars, sulfur-containing amino acids, and native polyunsaturated lipids—triggers a cascade of simultaneous, often fiercely competing, chemical reactions.

The central biochemical challenge in plant protein processing is navigating what is fundamentally a flavor and safety paradox. The precise thermal conditions required to generate highly desirable, meat-like sulfurous volatiles such as 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol are the exact same conditions that initiate the oxidative cleavage of native polyunsaturated fatty acids. This lipid degradation yields undesirable, beany, and grassy aldehydes and furans, notably hexanal and 2-pentylfuran. Furthermore, these elevated processing temperatures, which typically exceed the critical 120°C threshold during extrusion and baking, provide the requisite activation energy for free asparagine to react with reducing sugars or lipid-derived carbonyls. This reaction inevitably yields the probable human carcinogen acrylamide, presenting a significant toxicological hurdle.

Consequently, the optimization of plant-based meat analogue organoleptic properties cannot be achieved through the rudimentary addition of flavor precursors. It demands a highly nuanced, quantifiable understanding of kinetic reaction rates, covalent protein-flavor binding mechanisms, and the thermodynamic stability of the protein's tertiary and quaternary structures under thermal stress. This comprehensive report provides an exhaustive, data-driven analysis of the volatile, structural, and toxicological transformations occurring within heated soy and pea protein matrices. By examining the absolute headspace concentrations of target odorants, the release efficiencies of volatile thiols, the kinetics of acrylamide formation, the dynamic variations in free sulfhydryl and free amino groups, and the simultaneous quantification of competing reaction pathways, a rigorous mechanistic framework is established for the advancement of plant-based food science.

## **Absolute Headspace Quantification and Synthesis of Sulfur-Containing Volatiles**

The generation of authentic meaty, savory, and roasted aromas in plant-based matrices is heavily reliant on the Maillard reaction, specifically through the thermal degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids such as L-cysteine in the presence of reducing sugars like ribose, xylose, or glucose. Among the most potent and sensorially impactful odorants generated via these pathways are the furanthiols: 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol. Both of these compounds possess exceptionally low odor detection thresholds, with 2-methyl-3-furanthiol exhibiting an odor threshold in the parts-per-trillion range, imparting intense beefy and roasted notes to the food matrix.1

When soy protein isolate is utilized as a base matrix, the addition of exogenous cysteine and ribose followed by thermal treatment initiates highly specific degradation pathways governed by the precursor carbon skeletons. The rigorous quantification of these resulting volatiles, however, presents significant analytical challenges. Current scientific literature demonstrates a notable gap regarding the establishment of definitive, absolute headspace concentrations (expressed in parts-per-billion or micrograms per kilogram) of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol, 2-furfurylthiol, hexanal, and 2-pentylfuran measured simultaneously in a singular, strictly controlled matrix of soy protein isolate heated explicitly with added cysteine and ribose. Despite this specific parametric limitation, an extensive review of isotopic labeling studies, model aqueous systems, and complex fermented soy matrices provides a robust extrapolation of the expected concentration dynamics and synthetic pathways.

Isotope labeling studies utilizing carbon-13 enriched ribose combined with headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled to gas chromatography-mass spectrometry have conclusively elucidated the exact mechanistic pathways of furanthiol generation. In controlled experimental models consisting of phosphate-buffered solutions maintained at a pH of 5.0 and heated at 95°C for 4 hours, the reaction between cysteine and a mixture of natural and \[13C5\]ribose was meticulously tracked.3 The mass spectrometric data indicated that the fragmentation of ribose does not play a significant role in the formation of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol or 2-furfurylthiol. Instead, the carbon skeleton of the ribose molecule remains entirely intact to form the methylfuran moiety of the resulting thiol compounds.3 The reaction mechanism predominantly bypasses traditional intermediate structures, such as 4-hydroxy-5-methyl-3(2H)-furanone. Rather, the ribose sugar converts directly to a 1,4-dideoxyosone intermediate, which subsequently reacts with hydrogen sulfide derived from the Strecker degradation of the cysteine precursor to yield 2-methyl-3-furanthiol.3 Similarly, 2-furfurylthiol is generated primarily through a furfural intermediate reacting with cysteine derivatives.4

While the exact absolute concentrations in a pure soy protein isolate-cysteine-ribose system remain elusive in isolated literature, the absolute quantities of these compounds have been successfully mapped in closely related complex soy-based matrices. In fermented soy sauce models, where endogenous microbial activity provides both the necessary amino acid precursors and the enzymatic pre-conditioning, thermal treatment at 120°C drives the generation of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol to absolute concentrations that vastly exceed its perception threshold. In these systems, even the marginal addition of 0.2 micrograms per liter (µg/L) of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol to the heated soy matrix significantly intensifies the cooked meat-like aroma.6 Furthermore, in comparative analyses of Chinese soy sauce aroma-type liquors, the odor activity values of these two specific thiols reach remarkably high absolute levels, ranging from 256 to 263, allowing them to serve as definitive chemical markers for the differentiation of complex soy-derived aromatic profiles.7

The parallel generation of off-flavor compounds, specifically hexanal and 2-pentylfuran, occurs concurrently in these heated systems due to the autoxidation of residual lipids bound to the soy protein matrix. While the cysteine and ribose drive the sulfurous Maillard chemistry, the native linoleic acid fractions undergo rapid degradation. In unheated, commodity-grade soy protein isolates (for example, the commercial SUPRO 760 variant), the initial absolute concentration of hexanal has been rigorously quantified at 171 micrograms per kilogram (µg/kg), with 2-pentylfuran present at approximately 59.7 µg/kg.8 These absolute values are typically determined using internal standards, such as heptanone spiked at 164 µg/kg within a 5% aqueous protein slurry, to ensure precise quantification despite the matrix effects.8 When thermal energy is introduced to catalyze the cysteine-ribose reaction, the concentrations of these lipid-derived aldehydes and furans initially spike due to accelerated hydroperoxide cleavage, before eventually participating in secondary Schiff base reactions that complicate their absolute headspace recovery.

## **Headspace Release Efficiency and Binding Retention of Volatile Thiols**

The actual sensory perception of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol in plant-based protein solutions is heavily constrained by the headspace release efficiency of the matrix. Plant proteins, particularly the globular structures of soy and pea isolates, exhibit a profound "flavor-sink" effect. This phenomenon severely diminishes the percentage of volatile thiols recovered in the headspace when compared to protein-free aqueous controls subjected to identical thermal and environmental conditions.

The retention of these potent sulfurous odorants in aqueous plant protein solutions is driven by a complex interplay of both irreversible covalent binding mechanisms and reversible non-covalent interactions, such as hydrophobic entrapment and hydrogen bonding. High-molecular-weight protein fractions, predominantly the 7S β-conglycinin and 11S glycinin in soy, and the legumin and vicilin fractions in pea, provide abundant binding sites. When the protein matrices are heated, their native folded structures denature, exposing these sites and causing the release efficiency of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol to plummet dramatically.

Two primary mechanisms govern this robust covalent retention. The first is thiol-disulfide exchange. Thermally denatured plant proteins expose free sulfhydryl groups originating from the native cysteine residues buried within their hydrophobic cores. Odor-active thiols like 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol undergo rapid and largely irreversible disulfide interchange reactions with these newly exposed, protein-bound sulfhydryl groups. This specific chemical reaction effectively tethers the flavor molecule directly to the protein's macro-structure, removing it from the volatile headspace.9

The second, and perhaps more profound mechanism affecting headspace release efficiency, is melanoidin trapping. During the extended heating of these protein solutions, the parallel Maillard reaction generates high-molecular-weight, brown-colored melanoidins and highly reactive pyrazinium intermediates, specifically CROSSPY-type radicals. Both 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol rapidly form covalent bonds with these intermediates. Advanced kinetic studies tracking the stability of these thiols have demonstrated that the presence of melanoidins and protein-derived Maillard complexes can decrease the measurable headspace concentration of 2-furfurylthiol by a staggering factor of 16 when compared to a pristine aqueous control.10 This massive reduction equates to a retention rate exceeding 93%, illustrating just how little of the synthesized flavor actually escapes the matrix to reach the olfactory receptors.

The binding retention of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol is further complicated by the simultaneous presence of lipid oxidation products within the soy or pea protein solution. Aldehydes, such as hexanal, and various alkenals aggressively compete for the same binding sites on the denaturing protein. While aldehydes predominantly form Schiff bases through nucleophilic attack on the free amino groups of the protein, α,β-unsaturated aldehydes can also undergo Michael additions directly with the free sulfhydryl groups.11 As the total number of exposed sulfhydryl groups decreases over the initial 10 minutes of intense heating—driven both by natural protein-protein crosslinking and competitive aldehyde binding—the protein's theoretical capacity to covalently bind 2-methyl-3-furanthiol drops. Theoretically, this should lead to an increase in the headspace release efficiency of the thiol. However, the concurrent thermal aggregation and eventual gelation of the protein matrix physically entrap the remaining volatile molecules within the interstitial spaces of the newly formed hydrogel network. Consequently, the overall headspace recovery of these highly desirable, meaty, sulfurous notes in plant-based systems remains remarkably poor, requiring extreme over-dosing of precursors to achieve the desired sensory effect.11

## **Quantitative Structural Mapping: Free Sulfhydryl and Amino Group Dynamics**

To accurately quantify the structural changes that dictate flavor binding, gelation, and texture formation in heated soy and pea protein isolates, researchers rely heavily on two primary spectrophotometric methodologies: Ellman’s reagent assay for the determination of free sulfhydryl groups, and the ortho-phthaldialdehyde assay for the quantification of free amino groups. The absolute measurements of these functional groups, conducted without the confounding variables of enzymatic hydrolysis, serve as direct, quantifiable indicators of protein unfolding, progressive oxidation, and the extent of Maillard conjugation.

### **Free Sulfhydryl Group Variations via Ellman's Assay**

The quantification of free sulfhydryl (-SH) groups utilizes 5,5'-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid), commonly known as DTNB or Ellman's reagent. When DTNB reacts with free aliphatic thiol groups on the protein backbone, it undergoes cleavage of its disulfide bond to yield 2-nitro-5-thiobenzoate, a yellow-colored anion that strongly absorbs light at 412 nm, allowing for precise molar quantification.

In native, unheated soy protein isolate, the baseline total free sulfhydryl content is typically measured at approximately 8.0 micromoles per gram (µmol/g) of protein.12 However, the application of thermal and mechanical treatments drastically alters this baseline through distinct kinetic phases. When soy protein isolate is subjected to moderate, traditional wet heating (for instance, holding a 2% w/v solution at 70°C for 10 minutes at standard atmospheric pressure of 0.1 MPa), the globular proteins begin to unfold. This thermal denaturation exposes the previously buried cysteine residues to the aqueous environment, resulting in a transient but highly significant increase in detectable surface free sulfhydryl (S-SH) groups. Under these exact parameters, absolute values of surface free sulfhydryl groups have been recorded peaking at 12.12 ± 0.50 µmol/g.13 Similarly, when external mechanical energy, such as high-intensity ultrasonication, is applied to related legume isolates like chickpea protein, the ensuing cavitation forces severely disrupt the tertiary structure, driving the free sulfhydryl content from a baseline of 5.00 µmol/g up to an exposed 14.31 µmol/g.15

However, when the thermal treatment is prolonged, or when heat is combined with extreme pressures (such as high-pressure homogenization) or highly oxidative environments, a secondary kinetic phase rapidly takes over. The newly exposed free sulfhydryl groups undergo rapid oxidation and participate in extensive intra- and inter-molecular disulfide (-S-S-) bond formation. Consequently, the absolute free sulfhydryl content drops sharply. For example, when soy protein isolate is subjected to high-pressure homogenization at 140 MPa, the total sulfhydryl (T-SH) content plunges to 13.28 ± 0.28 µmol/g from its higher transient peaks, reflecting the massive conversion of free thiols into structural disulfide bridges.13 In whey protein models utilized for comparative stability, free sulfhydryl losses of up to 44% to 58.06% have been recorded following intense thermal and oxidative treatments, showcasing the universal reactivity of these groups across diverse protein types.16 This precipitous drop in free SH groups correlates directly with the onset of protein aggregation and gelation, where the newly formed disulfide linkages create the rigid, cross-linked polymer networks fundamentally necessary for achieving meat-like texture.19

### **Free Amino Group Variations via the Ortho-Phthaldialdehyde Assay**

The ortho-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) assay is utilized to precisely quantify the free amino groups present in the protein matrix, primarily targeting the ε-amino groups of lysine residues and the α-amino groups located at the N-termini of the polypeptide chains. In heated plant protein systems containing reducing sugars, the absolute concentration of free amino groups operates as a vital, inverse proxy for the extent of the Maillard reaction, effectively measuring the degree of protein glycation.

When soy or pea protein isolates are heated in the presence of carbohydrates, a nucleophilic attack by the unprotonated free amino groups on the electrophilic carbonyl carbons of the reducing sugars initiates the rapid formation of Schiff bases. This continuous consumption of available \-NH2 groups leads to a statistically significant, measurable reduction in OPA reactivity. While native, unreacted proteins exhibit high baseline OPA absorbance, thermal treatments designed to maximize Maillard conjugation—such as sustained heating at 90°C for 5 to 10 minutes—show progressive, quantifiable declines in free amino group concentration.19

In advanced studies involving the interaction between plant proteins and oxidative compounds, the loss of these amino groups serves as a marker for both non-enzymatic browning and protein cross-linking. The depletion of these free amino sites not only reduces the nutritional bioavailability of essential amino acids like lysine but fundamentally alters the isoelectric point and surface hydrophobicity of the protein. This chemical modification subsequently modifies the protein's macroscopic functionality, altering its water-holding capacity, emulsification stability, and ultimate gelling behavior.19

## **Kinetic Modeling of Volatile Release at Multiple Time Points**

Traditional endpoint analysis provides a severely limited, static view of flavor chemistry. Therefore, dynamic kinetic studies measuring volatile concentrations at multiple, specific time points during a continuous reaction are absolutely crucial for mapping the transient peaks of both desirable meat-like compounds and undesirable lipid oxidation products.

However, comprehensive literature reviews reveal a severe scarcity of continuous time-series data mapping volatile formation strictly within unhydrolyzed soy or pea protein isolate matrices during heating. Most existing research relies heavily on single-point endpoint analyses to determine final flavor profiles. Despite this limitation, robust kinetic data has been generated using highly controlled model systems and closely related complex matrices, providing a clear window into the temporal evolution of these volatiles.

In aqueous simulated solutions heated at varying temperatures between 90°C and 130°C, the kinetics of volatile formation consistently follow a multi-step, non-linear progression.21 Time-course sampling conducted across extensive intervals, such as from 0 to 360 minutes, demonstrates that primary lipid oxidation products like hexanal appear remarkably early in the thermal cycle. This rapid emergence is driven by the low activation energy required for the decomposition of pre-existing lipid hydroperoxides natively present in the plant matrix. Hexanal concentrations typically spike within the initial heating phase, rapidly reaching maximum concentration before plateauing or slowly declining. This subsequent decline is attributed to hexanal undergoing secondary chemical reactions, such as Schiff base formation with the surrounding protein amines, or participating in complex aldol condensations.22

Conversely, the generation of Maillard-derived products—such as Strecker aldehydes, pyrazines, furanones, and the critical sulfur-containing thiols—exhibits a distinct and measurable lag phase. The initial stages of the Maillard reaction, primarily the Amadori rearrangement, produce non-volatile, odorless intermediates. It is only after extended thermal exposure that these Amadori products degrade into the volatile species responsible for roasted and meaty aromas. Consequently, meticulous time-point sampling reveals an intersecting concentration curve: lipid oxidation volatiles absolutely dominate the early temporal window of thermal processing, while the robust generation of Maillard products dominates the later stages.24 Advanced studies utilizing gas chromatography-mass spectrometry combined with precisely calibrated internal standards—such as cycloleucine, heavy-water isotopes, or specific matrix-matched reference materials like heptanone—allow for the absolute quantification of these shifting kinetics, ensuring that the transient peaks of highly reactive intermediates are accurately captured before they degrade or covalently bind to the matrix.8

## **Thermal Dynamics of Hexanal and 2-Pentylfuran Sequestration (90–130°C)**

The absolute concentrations of the primary off-flavor compounds hexanal and 2-pentylfuran in soy and pea protein aqueous suspensions are heavily modulated by the exact parameters of the applied thermal treatment, specifically within the critical processing window of 90°C to 130°C.

In their initial, unheated state, commercially available soy protein isolates exhibit significant baseline accumulations of these lipid oxidation products. For instance, in commodity-grade SUPRO 760 soy protein isolate analyzed as a 5% aqueous slurry, initial absolute hexanal concentrations have been quantified at a remarkably high 171 µg/kg (parts-per-billion). Concurrently, 2-pentylfuran, which imparts a stale, cardboard-like odor, is present at initial concentrations of approximately 59.7 µg/kg.8 These initial levels far exceed the human sensory detection thresholds, necessitating thermal intervention.

When these suspensions are subjected to heating in the targeted 90°C to 130°C range, a highly complex kinetic behavior is observed, characterized by simultaneous physical release and chemical sequestration. Initially, as temperatures approach the 90°C to 92°C mark, the 11S glycinin fraction—a major, thermally stable structural component of both soy and pea proteins—undergoes significant thermal denaturation.22 This macroscopic unfolding exposes previously buried hydrophobic core regions, triggering a temporary release or "flash" of physically bound hexanal and 2-pentylfuran into the headspace.

However, as the heating duration extends within the sub-boiling to boiling range, the absolute free concentrations of these aldehydes often paradoxically decrease. In-depth mechanistic research indicates that holding a protein slurry at 80°C to 90°C for 60 minutes facilitates a permanent condensation reaction between the volatile hexanal and the newly exposed basic amino acid residues on the protein backbone. Specifically, the hexanal molecule executes a nucleophilic attack on the Lys74 residue of the acidic subunit of the 11S glycinin protein.22 This reaction forms a stable Schiff base, covalently sequestering the hexanal and physically shifting the molecular weight of the protein subunit by approximately \+84 Daltons.22 This indicates that moderate thermal processing can effectively act as a chemical sink, covalently locking away off-flavors and reducing their post-treatment headspace concentrations.

If processing temperatures are pushed higher, penetrating the 120°C to 130°C threshold typical of extrusion or sterilization, a different mitigation mechanism takes precedence. Extreme, rapid heat leads to the aggressive volatilization and subsequent thermal degradation of both hexanal and 2-pentylfuran. For example, high-temperature pretreatment at 121°C for just 3 minutes has been shown to successfully "flash off" these compounds, significantly reducing the post-treatment concentrations of beany off-flavors in soy protein isolate prior to further formulation.27

## **Acrylamide Formation Kinetics in Plant Protein Matrices**

While optimizing thermal processing to encourage Maillard browning and mitigate lipid off-flavors is essential for sensory quality, it carries the severe toxicological risk of generating acrylamide. Acrylamide, classified as a Group 2A probable human carcinogen, is formed almost exclusively via the specific reaction of the free amino acid asparagine with reducing sugars, or highly reactive carbonyls derived from lipid oxidation, at elevated processing temperatures.

The thermodynamic threshold for significant acrylamide formation is strictly bounded by temperature. Extensive kinetic profiling demonstrates that the synthesis of acrylamide requires temperatures exceeding 120°C to proceed at any measurable rate.29 Below this critical temperature, such as during the gentle simmering or boiling of plant protein suspensions in aqueous environments, acrylamide formation is essentially negligible. However, as processing temperatures rise into the ranges required for extrusion, baking, or frying (typically 130°C to 180°C), the nucleophilic addition reaction between the reducing sugars and asparagine accelerates exponentially.30

Time acts as an equally critical variable in this formation kinetic. In both starchy and highly proteinaceous matrices subjected to baking at 180°C, short processing durations yield relatively low, manageable acrylamide concentrations, generally hovering between 20 and 50 µg/kg.30 However, extended thermal exposure at higher temperatures, pushing up to 220°C, drives the concentrations sharply upward, yielding values between 100 and 200 µg/kg, and occasionally scaling much higher depending on the specific precursor availability within the matrix.30 Interestingly, comprehensive thermodynamic models suggest that at extreme temperatures exceeding 200°C, the rate of acrylamide thermal degradation and polymerization can eventually outpace its rate of formation, leading to a late-stage decline in total absolute concentration.31

The inherent composition of soy and pea protein isolates significantly dictates their individual acrylamide-forming potential. Soybean matrices, particularly byproducts such as soy okara, naturally contain exceptionally high baseline levels of both free asparagine and reducing sugars. When these matrices are utilized in high-heat applications like cookies, they yield pronounced acrylamide concentrations, cresting at 588.84 µg/kg.29

Crucially, the formation of acrylamide does not occur in chemical isolation; it is subject to intense precursor competition from the surrounding protein matrix. Rigorous statistical correlation analyses of various plant-based meat analogues demonstrate a strong, definitive *negative* correlation between ultimate acrylamide levels and the native concentrations of total protein and specific free amino acids, including aspartic acid, threonine, valine, arginine, and lysine.29 Because lysine competes directly and aggressively with asparagine for available dicarbonyl compounds to form Advanced Glycation End-products (AGEs), such as Nε-(carboxymethyl)lysine and Nε-(carboxyethyl)lysine, fortifying the protein matrix with free lysine can actively suppress the ultimate yield of acrylamide.29

Furthermore, the native lipid content fundamentally alters the kinetic pathways. Unsaturated free fatty acids exhibit a complex, pH-dependent influence on acrylamide synthesis. At an acidic pH of 4.6, unsaturated free fatty acids actually induce greater acrylamide formation by providing a wealth of secondary carbonyl precursors derived from lipid breakdown. Conversely, at a more neutral pH of 6.0, these same fatty acids serve to inhibit acrylamide formation, highlighting the profound complexity of formulating safe plant-based foods.29

## **Simultaneous Chemical Cross-Talk: Maillard vs. Lipid Oxidation Pathways**

A truly comprehensive understanding of plant protein flavor chemistry requires examining the simultaneous, highly dynamic interplay between the Maillard reaction and lipid oxidation. These two fundamental chemical cascades are inextricably linked; they share molecular precursors, compete for transient intermediates, and actively modify each other's terminal products.

Studies that successfully execute the simultaneous quantification of both lipid oxidation products (such as hexanal, 2-pentylfuran, and 1-octen-3-ol) alongside Maillard reaction products (including pyrazines, furanones, and sulfur-containing volatiles) within the exact same plant protein sample provide the clearest illumination of this chemical cross-talk. For instance, in the rigorous instrumental and sensory profiling of roasted peas (*Pisum sativum* L.), hexanal was identified as a dominant, persistent lipid oxidation marker with a massive Odor Activity Value of 280, defining the baseline "green and beany" aromatic note. Concurrently, within that exact same sample, the thermal roasting process successfully synthesized a diverse, quantifiable suite of pyrazines and pyranones that superimposed a highly desirable "nutty" flavor onto the matrix.23

Similarly, deep analytical investigations into the uncharacterized "old soap" off-flavor frequently detected in plant-based meat analogues revealed a direct intersection of these pathways. It was discovered that the aldol condensation of primary lipid oxidation products—specifically pentanal and hexanal—generates highly odor-active α,β-unsaturated aldehydes. These secondary condensation products were successfully quantified across multiple, simultaneously tested matrices, including pea, soy, and rice protein isolates, proving that the extraction and drying phases of plant protein production inadvertently fuel this specific degradative chemistry.23

The chemical relationship between these two dominant pathways is highly antagonistic. The intermediates of the Maillard reaction, particularly the high-molecular-weight melanoidins and early-stage Amadori rearrangement products, exhibit potent free-radical scavenging abilities. Consequently, as the Maillard reaction progresses during the heating of soy or pea proteins, it actively retards the propagation phase of lipid autoxidation. This built-in antioxidant effect directly suppresses the secondary formation of undesirable aldehydes like hexanal and 2-pentylfuran, essentially using the browning reaction to clean up the flavor profile.34

Conversely, lipid oxidation aggressively interferes with the progression of the Maillard reaction. The thermal degradation of lipid hydroperoxides yields highly reactive, short-chain carbonyl species, such as malondialdehyde and glyoxal. These lipid-derived carbonyls forcefully compete with the intended reducing sugars to react with the free amino groups (as tracked by the OPA assay) and the free sulfhydryl groups (as tracked by the Ellman assay) of the protein matrix.12 This cross-talk fundamentally alters the trajectory of volatile synthesis. For example, certain unsaturated aldehydes generated from lipid breakdown, such as (E)-2-heptenal and (E,E)-2,4-decadienal, have been shown to physically interact with Maillard intermediates, inhibiting critical Amadori rearrangements. Most detrimentally, this lipid interference directly suppresses the formation of the highly desirable, meat-like sulfur-heterocycles, including furanthiols and thiophenes.36

Thus, the presence of oxidized lipids in soy and pea protein isolates not only contributes direct, unpleasant off-flavors to the food matrix but also acts as a profound chemical blockade. This interference prevents the efficient generation of savory, meaty Maillard aromatics, even when the system is deliberately supplemented with optimal precursors like cysteine and ribose.

## **Synthesis and Strategic Outlook**

The thermal processing of soy and pea protein isolates represents a highly dynamic, interconnected physicochemical system governed by fiercely competing kinetic pathways. The generation of highly desirable, meat-like volatiles, such as 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 2-furfurylthiol, from the precise addition of cysteine and ribose is severely hampered by the very nature of the protein matrix itself, which acts as an unavoidable thermodynamic "flavor sink." Through irreversible disulfide interchange with heat-exposed free sulfhydryl groups, and extensive covalent bonding to melanoidin intermediates, the ultimate headspace release efficiency of these vital odorants is drastically curtailed.

Simultaneously, the effective mitigation of inherent, lipid-derived off-flavors such as hexanal and 2-pentylfuran requires incredibly precise thermal manipulation. While extreme heat exceeding 155°C can successfully volatilize these compounds, targeted sub-denaturation holding temperatures (e.g., 80°C to 90°C) can be strategically utilized to permanently sequester these aldehydes via Schiff base condensation with the protein's free amino groups.

However, the application of high thermal energy to these plant matrices introduces profound toxicological considerations. The natural abundance of free asparagine and reducing sugars in legume isolates provides the ideal stoichiometric conditions for acrylamide formation at processing temperatures exceeding the critical 120°C threshold. Because the chemical pathways for desirable flavor generation and toxicant formation rely on shared precursors, parallel reaction mechanics, and identical thermal activation energies, completely decoupling them remains a formidable scientific challenge.

Future optimization of plant-based protein matrices must move beyond the simple, empirical addition of flavor precursors. Strategic formulation will require precise pH modulation to favor specific, safe Maillard pathways over others, the utilization of competitive amino acids like lysine to safely divert reactive carbonyls away from asparagine, and the application of targeted enzymatic pre-treatments to comprehensively exhaust lipid precursors before high-heat extrusion occurs. By mastering the kinetic, thermodynamic, and compositional variables detailed in this analysis, the food science industry can engineer advanced plant proteins that successfully balance organoleptic authenticity with uncompromising safety.
