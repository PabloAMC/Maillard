# **Comprehensive Analysis of Thiamine Thermal Degradation and its Synergistic Contributions to Meat Aroma Genesis**

## **Introduction to Meat Aroma Genesis and the Role of Thiamine**

The genesis of cooked meat aroma represents one of the most intricate and multidimensional phenomena within the discipline of food chemistry. In its raw state, the skeletal muscle tissue of animals—whether derived from bovine, porcine, avian, or ovine species—is fundamentally devoid of the characteristic olfactory properties associated with meat, possessing only a faint, serum-like, or metallic odor.1 The transformation of this relatively neutral biological matrix into a highly palatable, sensory-rich food product is entirely contingent upon the application of thermal energy during cooking processes such as boiling, roasting, frying, or stewing.1 Heat acts as the primary catalyst for a vast network of highly dynamic, non-enzymatic chemical cascades that degrade the water-soluble and lipid-soluble precursor pools inherent in the muscle tissue.3 The resultant volatilome—comprising hundreds of distinct organic compounds—dictates the complex gustatory and olfactory sensations universally recognized as "meaty".2

The precursor reservoir in raw meat is highly diverse, encompassing free amino acids, small peptides, reducing sugars, glycogen, ribonucleotides, glycoproteins, lipids, and trace vitamins.1 During thermal processing, these precursors undergo multiple, often overlapping, reaction pathways. The classical Maillard reaction, which involves the condensation of reducing sugars with the free amino groups of amino acids, is historically cited as the foundational pathway for flavor generation.5 Concurrently, Strecker degradation transforms amino acids into volatile aldehydes, while the thermal oxidation of intramuscular lipids and phospholipids generates a wide array of aliphatic alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones that impart fatty and species-specific notes.1 However, while these pathways are critical, contemporary flavor chemistry has identified the thermal degradation of thiamine (vitamin B1) as an equally pivotal, independent precursor pathway responsible for the generation of some of the most potent and character-defining volatile organic compounds in the meat volatilome.1

Thiamine is an essential, water-soluble micronutrient ubiquitous in animal tissues, functioning biologically as a critical coenzyme in energy metabolism.9 From a flavor chemistry perspective, however, its unique molecular architecture makes it an unparalleled reservoir for reactive sulfur and nitrogen species.8 Under the aqueous and thermal conditions typical of cooking, the thiamine molecule fractures, unleashing highly reactive intermediates that rapidly recombine, cyclize, and oxidize to form an extraordinary diversity of heterocyclic odorants.1 These degradation products—which include thiols, sulfides, disulfides, furans, thiophenes, and thiazoles—are distinguished by their exceptionally low sensory detection thresholds, often perceptible to the human olfactory system at parts-per-trillion concentrations.5

Understanding the precise molecular mechanisms governing thiamine degradation, quantifying its thermodynamic and kinetic behavior across varied physical parameters (such as temperature, pH, and water activity), and elucidating its profound synergistic crosstalk with classical Maillard intermediates are imperative endeavors for modern food scientists.12 This knowledge is not only essential for the optimization of traditional meat processing and the manufacturing of concentrated process flavorings, but it has also become the paramount scientific hurdle in the rapidly expanding sector of plant-based meat analogues (PBMAs).13 The successful engineering of authentic, convincing meat flavors in entirely novel, non-animal matrices relies entirely on the precise modulation of these thermal degradation pathways.13 This report provides an exhaustive, peer-reviewed synthesis of the thermal degradation of thiamine, analyzing its mechanistic chemistry, quantitative kinetics, synergistic interactions, olfactory impacts, and contemporary regulatory frameworks.

## **Structural Chemistry and Primary Cleavage Mechanisms of Thiamine**

To fully comprehend the flavor-generating capacity of thiamine, one must first examine its fundamental structural chemistry. Thiamine is a bicyclic compound composed of a 4-amino-2-methylpyrimidine ring connected via a methylene bridge to a 5-(2-hydroxyethyl)-4-methylthiazole ring.8 This structure incorporates an amino group, a hydroxyl group, a quaternary nitrogen atom within the thiazole ring, and a highly reactive sulfur atom.8 In biological systems and raw meat, thiamine exists largely in phosphorylated forms (such as thiamine pyrophosphate), but thermal processing rapidly hydrolyzes these esters into free thiamine, which subsequently undergoes massive thermal degradation.9

The initiation of thermal degradation is a highly complex process dictated predominantly by the physical parameters of the cooking environment—specifically the temperature (typically 80°C to 180°C) and the pH of the aqueous medium (ranging from pH 4.0 to 8.0).12 The thiamine molecule is notoriously sensitive to thermal stress, particularly as the pH approaches neutrality or mild alkalinity.20 The thermal destruction of thiamine generally proceeds via two competitive primary pathways, which establish the foundational intermediate pool for all subsequent flavor generation.

### **Methylene Bridge Cleavage in Mildly Acidic Conditions**

In environments characterized by a mildly acidic to neutral pH (approximately pH 4.0 to 6.0), the degradation of thiamine is primarily initiated by the hydrolytic cleavage of the methylene bridge that connects the pyrimidine and thiazole rings.8 This heterolytic cleavage effectively bisects the molecule, liberating a pyrimidine derivative (often 4-amino-5-hydroxymethyl-2-methylpyrimidine) and the intact 5-(2-hydroxyethyl)-4-methylthiazole moiety.8

The liberation of the intact thiazole ring is a critical preliminary step. While the pyrimidine fraction can undergo further degradation, its contribution to the volatile aroma profile is generally considered negligible compared to the sulfur-containing thiazole fragment.22 The isolated 5-(2-hydroxyethyl)-4-methylthiazole is inherently unstable under continued thermal stress and begins to fracture, yielding hydrogen sulfide gas (![][image1]), elemental sulfur, 2-methylthiophene, and 4,5-dihydro-2-methylthiophene.22 The generation of these highly reactive sulfur species is the thermodynamic prerequisite for the synthesis of complex, meaty heterocycles.1

### **Thiazole Ring Opening in Neutral to Alkaline Conditions**

As the pH of the cooking medium shifts toward neutrality and into the alkaline range (pH 6.5 to 8.0), the degradation pathway undergoes a fundamental mechanistic shift. Under these conditions, the base-catalyzed opening of the thiazole ring often precedes the cleavage of the methylene bridge.22 The quaternary nitrogen of the thiazole ring becomes highly susceptible to nucleophilic attack by hydroxide ions present in the aqueous solvent. This nucleophilic substitution leads to the rupture of the carbon-sulfur or carbon-nitrogen bonds within the five-membered ring, generating transient acyclic thiols and enolates.22

These acyclic intermediates rapidly decompose, releasing vast quantities of hydrogen sulfide, ammonia (![][image2]), and various aliphatic aldehydes and ketones into the reaction matrix.22 The simultaneous release of sulfur and nitrogen species into a heated matrix containing carbohydrate degradation products creates the perfect chemical incubator for the generation of complex, multi-substituted thiazoles and thiophenes, which characterize the aroma of roasted and severely heat-treated meats.22

## **Mechanistic Pathways to Key Sulfur-Containing Volatiles**

The secondary and tertiary reactions of the cleavage products detailed above generate the critical organoleptic compounds associated with meat aroma. The aqueous cooking of meat provides a highly reactive environment where intermediate thiols, sulfides, and dicarbonyls collide, undergo condensation, dehydrate, and cyclize. Through rigorous model studies utilizing gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and isotopic labeling, flavor chemists have elucidated the precise mechanisms responsible for the formation of the most impactful thiamine-derived volatiles.12

### **Formation of 2-Methyl-3-furanthiol (MFT)**

Of all the compounds identified in the meat volatilome, 2-methyl-3-furanthiol (MFT) is universally acknowledged as one of the most potent and fundamental contributors to the characteristic aroma of cooked beef, pork, and chicken broth.11 The aroma of pure MFT is intensely meaty, savory, and reminiscent of roasted meat and savory broths.11

The dominant mechanistic pathway for the generation of MFT from thiamine relies on the formation of a specific straight-chain intermediate: 5-hydroxy-3-mercapto-2-pentanone.29 When the thiazole ring of thiamine breaks down, it can yield this specific mercapto-ketone structure. Under the application of continuous thermal energy (typically between 100°C and 140°C in aqueous systems), 5-hydroxy-3-mercapto-2-pentanone undergoes a rapid intramolecular cyclization.23

The mechanism involves the nucleophilic attack of the terminal hydroxyl oxygen atom onto the electrophilic carbonyl carbon at the opposite end of the intermediate chain. This nucleophilic addition forms a cyclic hemiketal, which is highly unstable. Subsequent dehydration (the loss of a water molecule) drives the aromatization of the newly formed five-membered oxygen-containing ring, resulting in the generation of the highly stable furan structure with a sulfhydryl group attached at the third position—yielding 2-methyl-3-furanthiol.23 The yield of this specific compound is heavily dependent on precise processing conditions; for instance, quantitative model studies have demonstrated that the highest yields of MFT (approximately 1.4 mol%) are achieved when intermediate precursors are reacted for short durations (e.g., 6 minutes) at elevated temperatures (180°C).31

### **Dimerization to Bis(2-methyl-3-furyl)disulfide**

While MFT is a critical flavor compound in its monomeric state, it is chemically labile and highly susceptible to oxidation.11 The aqueous matrix of cooking meat—particularly red meat such as beef—is replete with pro-oxidant factors, including ambient oxygen dissolved in the water, reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as singlet oxygen (![][image3]) and superoxide radicals (![][image4]), and high concentrations of transition metals like iron (![][image5]) and copper (![][image6]) liberated from myoglobin and other metalloproteins.20

In the presence of these oxidative catalysts, MFT rapidly undergoes a radical-mediated dimerization process.11 The oxidation abstracts the hydrogen atom from the sulfhydryl (S-H) group of MFT, generating a highly reactive thiyl radical.32 When two such thiyl radicals collide in the reaction matrix, they covalently bond, forming a disulfide bridge. This homolytic radical recombination results in the formation of bis(2-methyl-3-furyl)disulfide.11

This dimerization is of paramount importance to meat flavor chemistry. The resulting disulfide possesses an even lower olfactory detection threshold than the monomeric MFT and imparts a distinct, heavy, "rich aged-beef" or "prime-rib" character that is crucial for the authenticity of beef aromas.34 Analytical evaluations utilizing sulfur-specific pulsed-flame photometric detection (PFPD) have confirmed that the conversion of MFT to its disulfide dimer happens rapidly during cooking and subsequent storage, with the dimer often dominating the aroma profile within hours of thermal processing.12

### **Synthesis of 2-Furfurylthiol (FFT)**

2-Furfurylthiol (FFT), a compound historically associated with the aroma of freshly roasted coffee, is also a vital constituent of cooked meat flavor, particularly in poultry and roasted pork.35 The aroma of FFT is characterized by intense roasted, savory, and burnt sugar notes.36

The generation of FFT from thiamine represents an interaction between the sulfur-donating capacity of the degraded vitamin and the carbohydrate-like structural remnants in the cooking matrix. When thiamine degrades, it liberates substantial quantities of hydrogen sulfide (![][image1]).22 Concurrently, the degradation of the pentose-like backbone structures inherent in the biological matrix generates furan-2-aldehyde, commonly known as furfural.31

The synthesis of FFT occurs via the nucleophilic substitution of the formyl oxygen of furfural by the highly nucleophilic sulfhydryl group derived from the dissolved ![][image1].36 While thiamine alone can serve as a complete precursor for FFT in highly concentrated model systems, kinetic studies indicate that the generation of FFT is extraordinarily efficient (up to ten times more rapid) when furan-2-aldehyde is directly reacted with an exogenous source of ![][image1], underscoring the role of thiamine primarily as a sulfur donor in this specific mechanistic pathway.31

### **Methylation to 2-Methyl-3-(methylthio)furan**

The complexity of the meat volatilome is further enhanced by secondary substitution reactions, most notably the methylation of furanthiols. 2-Methyl-3-(methylthio)furan is an odorant specifically identified as a character-impact compound for the aroma of roast beef.34 Unlike MFT, which imparts a boiled or savory broth note, the methylthio derivative is strongly associated with the crust of roasted meats.34

The exact mechanism of formation relies on the prior generation of MFT. Under the severe thermal conditions of roasting (temperatures exceeding 140°C) and the corresponding reduction in surface water activity (![][image7]), MFT undergoes alkylation.37 The high-temperature environment facilitates the generation of methyl-donating intermediates or methylthio radicals from parallel thermal degradation processes (such as the breakdown of methionine).2 The direct radical recombination of a methylthio radical with a furyl radical, or the electrophilic methylation of the MFT sulfhydryl group, yields 2-methyl-3-(methylthio)furan.37 The suppression of this compound in highly aqueous, boiled systems and its prominence in roasted systems highlights the profound impact of thermodynamic state on flavor synthesis.37

### **Generation of Thiophenes and Thiazolines**

The degradation of thiamine also yields a vast array of cyclic compounds where sulfur and nitrogen are integrated directly into the ring structure, namely thiophenes and thiazolines.

Thiophenes, such as 2-methylthiophene, 3-thiophenethiol, and 2-formyl-5-methylthiophene, contribute distinct meaty, earthy, and sometimes bready or burnt aromatic notes.1 These compounds are generated when the sulfur atom from the degraded thiazole ring is incorporated into a newly forming five-membered carbon ring.1 This occurs via the reaction of dissolved hydrogen sulfide with various dicarbonyl intermediates (derived from parallel sugar degradation), followed by complex dehydration and cyclization steps.1

Thiazolines and intact thiazoles (such as 2-acetyl-2-thiazoline, which imparts an intense roasty, popcorn-like aroma) represent the preservation or recombination of both the sulfur and nitrogen atoms originally present in thiamine.5 The mechanism involves the concurrent condensation of ![][image1] and ammonia (![][image2]) with ![][image8]\-dicarbonyls and aldehydes.24 The reaction proceeds through the formation of intermediate Schiff bases, which subsequently undergo nucleophilic attack by the sulfhydryl group, leading to ring closure and the formation of the highly stable thiazoline or thiazole heterocycle.24

### **The Profound Influence of Matrix pH**

The thermodynamic stability of the thiamine molecule is profoundly, and non-linearly, sensitive to the pH of the surrounding aqueous matrix.14 This sensitivity is driven by the protonation state of the pyrimidine ring. Thiamine features a food-relevant acid dissociation constant (![][image21]) of approximately 4.8.14

In highly acidic environments (pH values below 6.0, and particularly around pH 3.0 to 4.5), the nitrogen atom at position 1 (N1) of the pyrimidine ring is fully protonated.14 This protonation structurally stabilizes the entire bicyclic molecule against thermal cleavage.22 Kinetic analyses reveal that at pH 3.0, the activation energy (![][image18]) required for degradation is exceptionally high, ranging from 21 to 32 kcal/mol depending on the specific salt form (e.g., thiamine mononitrate versus thiamine chloride hydrochloride).38 In these stabilized acidic solutions, the observed rate constant ![][image12] is independent of the initial thiamine concentration, indicating a uniform degradation pathway dominated by a high thermal barrier.39 Under low-temperature storage conditions (25°C to 40°C), thiamine content in a pH 3.0 matrix remains impressively stable, with over 91% retention recorded even after 392 days of storage.39

Conversely, as the pH transitions toward mild acidity and neutrality (pH 6.0 to 8.0)—which encompasses the natural pH range of most post-mortem meat tissues—the predominant molecular species shifts to the unprotonated pyrimidine N1.14 The loss of this proton drastically destabilizes the molecule, rendering the methylene bridge and thiazole ring highly susceptible to nucleophilic attack and cleavage.39 At pH 6.0 and above, the activation energy drops significantly to between 18 and 21 kcal/mol, indicating a substantially lower energetic threshold for degradation.39 Furthermore, the degradation kinetics in neutral environments become highly dependent on the initial concentration of the precursor. For instance, empirical data demonstrates that at a constant temperature of 70°C, a low-concentration solution (1 mg/mL) exhibits a ![][image12] of 0.040–0.047 ![][image13], whereas a saturated solution (20 mg/mL) experiences vastly accelerated destruction with a ![][image12] of 0.38–0.43 ![][image13].39

### **The Impact of Buffers and Transition Metals**

The kinetics of degradation are further complicated by the specific ionic composition of the aqueous matrix. The type of buffer used in a system directly impacts stability. Research indicates that to optimize thiamine retention in low-pH beverage or broth systems, phosphate buffers provide greater stability than citrate buffers.42 However, this trend reverses in high-pH systems, where citrate buffers afford greater protection against thermal destruction than phosphate.42

The presence of transition metal ions—which are abundant in meat due to metalloproteins like myoglobin and hemoglobin—acts as a potent catalyst for thiamine destruction. Studies evaluating the effect of metal chlorides at 25°C revealed significant variations in thiamine loss over a 7-day period depending on the specific metal ion and its oxidation state. The introduction of Copper(I) chloride (![][image22]) resulted in a 64.00% loss of thiamine, whereas Iron(III) chloride (![][image23]) induced a more severe 78.34% loss.20 As temperatures increase to 55°C, the catalytic destruction accelerates, with ![][image22] and ![][image23] driving thiamine losses to 61.94% and 95.98%, respectively.20 This aggressive metal-catalyzed degradation highlights why thiamine breaks down so completely and rapidly in highly pigmented red meats like beef during cooking.

### **Water Activity (![][image7]) and Volatile Yield Profiles**

Water activity (![][image7]) critically modulates both the rate of thiamine degradation and the specific quantitative yield of the resulting volatiles. The activation energy for thiamine degradation generally decreases as water activity increases.40 However, the generation of specific character-impact compounds behaves non-linearly in response to available moisture.

In high-moisture, aqueous systems typical of boiling or stewing (![][image24]), the generation of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and its subsequent dimerization to bis(2-methyl-3-furyl)disulfide are highly favored.26 The high mobility of water-soluble intermediates allows for rapid cyclization and radical recombination in the aqueous phase.

Conversely, in low-moisture systems characteristic of roasting, baking, or frying (![][image25]), the physical dynamics change entirely.37 The lack of free water heavily favors intense dehydration reactions, which are necessary to form complex substituted thiophenes and thiazolines.1 Furthermore, the physical presence of a dense, low-moisture meat fiber matrix introduces profound steric constraints and mass-transfer limitations. In these systems, the overall quantitative yield of free volatiles in the headspace is significantly suppressed compared to free liquid systems.37 This suppression occurs because the highly reactive thiamine-derived thiols rapidly undergo covalent binding with structural muscle proteins, cross-linking with exposed sulfhydryl groups via disulfide bridges, thereby trapping the flavor compounds within the meat matrix.32

## **Synergistic Interactions with Maillard Reaction Intermediates**

While the thermal degradation of thiamine is a potent source of meat flavor in rigorous isolation, the true, profound complexity of the meat volatilome is a product of intense chemical synergy. During the thermal processing of meat, thiamine degradation runs concurrently and intimately with the Maillard reaction—a vast, chaotic network of non-enzymatic browning reactions occurring between reducing sugars (e.g., ribose, glucose, xylose) and free amino groups derived from amino acids and peptides (e.g., cysteine, methionine, lysine).5 The chemical interaction and collision between intermediates from these parallel pathways result in a dramatic amplification of meaty character compounds, yielding a flavor profile far more complex than either pathway could produce independently.1

### **The Cysteine-Ribose-Thiamine Crosstalk**

The post-mortem aging and subsequent cooking of muscle tissue releases a highly specific pool of precursors. The enzymatic breakdown of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and ribonucleotides, particularly inosine monophosphate (IMP), yields copious amounts of free ribose in the muscle tissue.4 Simultaneously, the thermal degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids, primarily cysteine and methionine, provides a rich source of free sulfhydryl groups, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia.7

The most remarkable chemical coincidence in meat flavor chemistry is that the Maillard reaction occurring between ribose and cysteine yields the exact same character-impact odorants as the standalone thermal degradation of thiamine.44

1. **Ribose Degradation:** Under thermal stress, the five-carbon ribose sugar undergoes complex dehydration and rearrangement to form the highly reactive intermediate 4-hydroxy-5-methyl-3(2H)-furanone, commonly known as norfuraneol.44  
2. **Cysteine Degradation:** Concurrently, cysteine undergoes Strecker degradation, releasing massive quantities of ![][image1] into the local matrix.26  
3. **Synthesis:** The norfuraneol intermediate actively reacts with the available ![][image1] to synthesize 2-methyl-3-furanthiol (MFT).44

When thiamine is introduced into this specific Maillard matrix, a synergistic explosion of flavor synthesis occurs. Thiamine acts as a highly efficient, accelerated donor of both ![][image1] and 5-hydroxy-3-mercapto-2-pentanone.23 The sulfur species liberated from the thiazole ring of thiamine aggressively intercept the norfuraneol generated from the degrading ribose.44 Furthermore, the intermediates provided directly by thiamine react with the ammonia liberated from the Strecker degradation of amino acids, dramatically accelerating the formation of complex thiazoles and thiophenes.23

### **Isotopic Labeling and Pathway Competition Modulated by pH**

The precise nature of this synergy has been elegantly mapped using stable isotope dilution assays and ![][image26]\-labeled carbohydrates. By introducing ![][image26]\-labeled xylose and ribose into model systems alongside unlabeled cysteine and thiamine, researchers have been able to trace the origin of the carbon skeletons in the resulting volatile compounds.19

These sophisticated analyses reveal that the pathways are intensely competitive and entirely orchestrated by the pH of the system. In low pH environments (pH 3.0 to 4.0), the formation of specific volatiles like 2-furaldehyde and 2-furfurylthiol (FFT) is heavily favored, and mass spectrometry of the isotopic labels clearly indicates that the carbon skeleton is sourced almost exclusively from the sugar molecules.19 The sugar degradation pathway is dominant.

However, as the pH shifts to the 5.5 to 6.0 range—which precisely mirrors the natural physiological pH of post-mortem meat—a massive shift occurs. At this pH, both the sugar degradation products and the thiamine degradation products contribute heavily and equally to the carbon skeletons of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and 3-mercapto-2-pentanone.19 The pathways merge, proving that multiple, independent formation mechanisms operate in parallel within cooking meat, resulting in the massive amplification of these specific meaty odorants.19

### **Modulation by the Lipid Matrix and Lipid Oxidation Crosstalk**

The synergy between the Maillard reaction and thiamine degradation is further modulated, and often suppressed, by the presence of the lipid matrix. The unique flavor profiles that distinguish beef from pork or chicken are heavily reliant on the specific fatty acid compositions of the animal's intramuscular fat, particularly the phospholipids.7 Phospholipids contain a high proportion of unsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as arachidonic acid and linoleic acid, which are highly susceptible to thermal auto-oxidation during cooking.7

The lipid oxidation process generates a massive influx of highly reactive aliphatic aldehydes, ketones, and epoxides (for example, the intense compound trans-4,5-epoxy-(E)-2-decenal derived from arachidic acid).7 These lipid-derived carbonyls act as aggressive scavengers in the reaction matrix. They actively intercept the sulfur intermediates generated from the Maillard-thiamine synergy.2

High fat content has been empirically shown to inhibit the formation of certain critical sulfur heterocycles, particularly 2-methyl-3-furanthiol, 2-furanmethanethiol, and various thiophenes.2 This suppression occurs because the highly reactive thiamine-derived thiols function as sacrificial antioxidants within the meat; they quench the lipid radicals, preventing further lipid oxidation.11 In doing so, the thiols are consumed, and the reaction pathways are forcibly shifted toward the synthesis of long-chain alkylthiazoles and substituted thiophenes.2 This lipid-thiamine-Maillard crosstalk is the fundamental chemical reason why different species of meat possess distinct flavors despite sharing identical water-soluble precursors.2

## **Odor Activity Values (OAVs) and Sensory Thresholds**

Within the discipline of flavor chemistry, the absolute concentration of a volatile compound detected by a mass spectrometer does not inherently correlate with its sensory impact on the human palate.10 A compound present in massive quantities may be completely odorless, while a compound present in microscopic trace amounts can dominate the entire aroma profile. The true significance of an odorant is quantified by its Odor Activity Value (OAV), which is mathematically defined as the ratio of the compound's absolute concentration in the food matrix divided by its sensory odor detection threshold in that specific medium (e.g., water, air, or a complex lipid matrix).10

The sulfur-containing heterocyclic volatiles derived from thiamine degradation are characterized by vanishingly low odor detection thresholds. These compounds are among the most potent naturally occurring odorants known to science, meaning that even the microscopic quantities generated during the brief thermal window of cooking exert a massive, disproportionate influence over the final aroma profile of the food.11

The human olfactory system exhibits an evolutionary, extreme sensitivity to furanthiols and disulfides, likely as a mechanism for identifying protein-rich, cooked foods. ### **OAVs and Flavor Dilution Factors in Meat Systems**

In applied sensory studies, researchers frequently utilize Gas Chromatography-Olfactometry (GC-O) coupled with Aroma Extract Dilution Analysis (AEDA). This methodology determines Flavor Dilution (FD) factors, which operate similarly to OAVs by indicating the sheer potency of an odorant upon successive mathematical dilutions of the aroma extract until the human assessor can no longer perceive the scent.27

The application of AEDA to different animal species reveals striking differences in the hierarchy of thiamine-derived volatiles. In cooked beef extracts, the FD factor for monomeric MFT is exceptionally high, typically registering at values ![][image27].27 However, the true character-impact champion in beef is the dimer, bis(2-methyl-3-furyl)disulfide, which exhibits staggering FD factors exceeding 2048\.27 This unequivocally indicates that the oxidative dimerization of MFT in the highly oxygenated, iron-rich environment of beef muscle creates the dominant, overriding aroma note.27

Conversely, analyses of white meat systems, such as cooked chicken broth, highlight a completely inverted sensory hierarchy. In chicken systems, monomeric MFT serves as the most vital compound, dominating the profile with FD factors reaching 1024\.27 The disulfide dimer plays a negligible role in poultry flavor, presenting an FD factor of ![][image28].27 This stark contrast is driven by the vast differences in redox potential and transition metal concentration (specifically myoglobin iron) between red and white meat, which prevents the rapid oxidative dimerization of MFT in poultry.27

In highly controlled, fermented model broth systems—such as fermented soy sauces or enzymatically hydrolyzed beef proteins formulated with added thiamine—the calculated OAVs directly mirror these findings. In these aqueous matrices, MFT regularly presents massive OAVs ranging from 34 to 121, while FFT demonstrates OAVs between 11 and 103, marking them as the undisputed flavor markers of the system.47 The synergistic addition of free ribonucleotides (like IMP) to these model systems effectively multiplies the OAV of these thiol compounds by providing an uninhibited pool of reactive pentoses, leading to a highly palatable, overwhelmingly savory sensory profile.4

## **Application in Plant-Based Meat Analogues (PBMA) and Regulatory Framework**

Over the past decade, the global food industry has experienced a massive paradigm shift toward sustainable, alternative protein sources, resulting in the rapid commercialization and widespread adoption of Plant-Based Meat Analogues (PBMAs).13 However, despite significant advancements in replicating the physical texture and fibrous structure of muscle tissue, closing the sensory gap between traditional meat and PBMAs remains the paramount challenge for product developers.16

The fundamental issue lies in the precursor matrix. Plant proteins—such as soy, pea, and wheat gluten—are biologically deficient in the endogenous precursor pool required to autonomously generate authentic meat aroma.48 They lack the correct stoichiometric ratios of ribose, free sulfur-containing amino acids, specific intramuscular phospholipids, and, crucially, thiamine.15 Consequently, when a consumer cooks a PBMA, the matrix produces distinct "beany," "green," or "cereal" off-notes rather than the expected rich, savory, and meaty profile.5

### **Thiamine as a Targeted Flavor Precursor in PBMA Formulation**

To circumvent this chemical deficiency and recreate the complex volatilome of animal tissue, food engineers frequently utilize concentrated "process flavorings" or seek to directly embed targeted flavor precursors directly into the plant matrix.16 Given its unparalleled capacity to generate extreme-impact sulfur odorants, thiamine is an ideal candidate for this application.53 By supplementing the PBMA formulation with exogenous thiamine (frequently in tandem with xylose or ribose sugars and isolated amino acids), manufacturers attempt to program the matrix. The objective is to ensure that the thermal degradation pathways outlined in this report are triggered precisely at the moment the consumer cooks the product, resulting in the fresh, localized release of MFT, FFT, and bis(2-methyl-3-furyl)disulfide directly in the frying pan.53

However, incorporating thiamine into PBMAs presents formidable biophysical and processing challenges:

1. **Extrusion-Induced Degradation:** The production of PBMAs frequently relies on High Moisture Extrusion (HME) to texturize amorphous plant proteins into organized, meat-like fibers.13 The extrusion process subjects the ingredients to extreme physical forces, including high temperatures (often exceeding 140°C to 180°C), massive pressure, and intense mechanical shear forces within the extruder barrel.13 If free thiamine is added to the formulation prior to extrusion, the severe thermodynamic conditions cause premature, complete thermal degradation of the vitamin.55  
2. **Flash-Off and Matrix Entrapment:** The volatile thiols generated during premature extrusion degradation are either immediately flashed off as steam upon exiting the high-pressure extruder die, or they become permanently trapped within the dense plant matrix.32 Thiamine-derived thiols (like FFT and MFT) possess a notoriously high affinity for plant macromolecules and melanoidins.32 They readily form permanent covalent bonds—such as cross-linking disulfide bridges—with the reactive sulfhydryl groups exposed on the denatured plant globulins during extrusion.32 Consequently, despite the inclusion of high concentrations of thiamine precursors, the available OAV upon final consumer cooking remains sub-threshold, as the odorants are chemically locked into the structural proteins.32

To overcome these hurdles, modern flavor houses must employ sophisticated encapsulation technologies, utilizing lipid or carbohydrate matrices to physically shield the thiamine payload from the shear and heat of extrusion.39 Furthermore, precise buffering of the PBMA matrix to an optimal pH (approximately pH 5.5 to 6.0) is strictly required to stabilize the pyrimidine ring of the thiamine salt, protecting the precursor until the final, high-heat pan-frying or grilling stage is initiated by the end-user.39

### **Global Regulatory Status of Thiamine as a Flavor Precursor**

The strategic use of thiamine as a flavor precursor in novel food matrices is highly feasible from a commercial standpoint, driven by its well-established, impeccable safety profile and highly favorable regulatory status across all major global food jurisdictions.57

**United States Regulatory Framework (FDA):** Under the jurisdiction of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), thiamine (in both its commercial forms: thiamine hydrochloride and thiamine mononitrate) is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS).60 Its inclusion in food systems is legally sanctioned under multiple functional categories, serving both as an essential nutritional fortificant and actively as a flavor precursor.52 The Select Committee on GRAS Substances (SCOGS) has historically conducted exhaustive toxicological evaluations on thiamine salts. The committee definitively concluded that there is no biological basis to suspect any hazard to public health from the consumption of thiamine at existing or reasonably expected inclusion levels.60 This GRAS status entirely bypasses the arduous, multi-year pre-market approval processes that typically hinder the commercialization of novel, synthetic odorants.61

**European Union Regulatory Framework (EFSA):** In the European Union, the regulatory landscape is defined by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and is strictly governed by Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008, which dictates the use of flavorings and food ingredients with flavoring properties.64 Under the definitions set forth in Article 3 of this regulation, a "flavour precursor" is explicitly recognized and legally defined as a product—not necessarily possessing inherent flavoring properties itself—that is intentionally added to food for the sole, explicit purpose of producing flavor by breaking down or reacting with other components during food processing.65

Thiamine perfectly fulfills this legal definition. Furthermore, thiamine hydrochloride is officially registered on the EU Union List of Flavourings (designated as FL No. 16.030) and is fully authorized for use without stringent maximum inclusion limits.65 The European Food Safety Authority has repeatedly and independently assessed the toxicological profile of various thiamine salts used as nutritional and technological additives. These comprehensive evaluations have consistently confirmed that thiamine salts remain completely safe for human consumers, animal feed applications, and the broader environment, lacking any acute or chronic toxicity, though concentrated powders may act as mild skin irritants for factory workers.58

The intersection of extreme flavoring potency, natural biological origin, and universal regulatory approval positions thiamine as an indispensable tool in the arsenal of the modern flavor chemist. As the demand for authentic, sustainable plant-based proteins continues to escalate, the mastery of thiamine degradation kinetics will remain the critical scientific fulcrum upon which the success of the next generation of meat analogues balances.
