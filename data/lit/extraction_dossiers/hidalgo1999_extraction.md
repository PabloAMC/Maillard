### *"Effect of pH and Temperature on Comparative Nonenzymatic Browning of Proteins Produced by Oxidized Lipids and Carbohydrates"*

## 1. THE ONE-PARAGRAPH ANSWER

Ribose and oxidised linoleate products brown a protein in the same way (similar colour, fluorescence
and Ehrlich-adduct spectra), but under different conditions. At 25–50 °C the oxidised lipids (HP, SP)
cause more colour, more lysine and arginine loss and more pyrrolisation than ribose; at 80–120 °C ribose
causes the most (lysine loss at 120 °C: RI 75 %, SP 63 %, HP 42 %). At 80 °C all three treatments lose
the most lysine at **pH 7**, then pH 10, then pH 4. ΔE is inversely correlated with lysine recovered
(r = −0.93 to −0.98). The authors read the two routes as complementary sources of melanoidin-like
polypyrroles: lipid-derived browning where carbohydrate browning is slow.

| SP | 0.206 | 0.435 | 0.293 | **+0.33** | +0.17 |

**Stoichiometry check.** 1 mg/mL BSA carries ~0.88 µmol lysine/mL, i.e. **~0.9 mM lysine residues
against 10 mM carbonyl reagent**. Even the largest SP loss (63 % at 120 °C) consumes ≤ 0.6 mM, i.e.
**≤ 6 % of the SP dose** if each lysine took one SP molecule. The experiment is in **carbonyl excess**: it
measures how fast the protein is damaged, not how much aldehyde the protein removes.

## 5. USE IN THIS REPO

**Question it was downloaded for (L1 scoping):** does pH or sugar change how the protein's amines trap
lipid carbonyls, and could that move hexanal / nonanal / dienals across the P3 levers?

- **Mechanism pointer: yes, and pH-specific.** Secondary lipid oxidation products (aldehydes and
  epoxyalkenals) consume protein lysine and arginine at 80 °C, maximally at pH 7, with 2.1× (+0.33 dex)
  more lysine lost at pH 7 than at pH 4 and 1.5× (+0.17 dex) more than at pH 10. This is the
  carbonyl–amine `COVALENT_SINK` channel the lipid lane carries (inert by ruling), with a pH dependence
  that the lane does not have.
- **Not an aldehyde ratio.** The readout is lysine damage in carbonyl excess (§4). In the P3 pot the
  regime is reversed: 15 % pea isolate carries ~0.07 M lysine residues (estimate in
  `hidalgo2006_extraction.md` §5) against µM–mM lipid aldehydes, so the protein is in amine excess and
  what matters is the pseudo-first-order trapping rate. If the pH dependence of the rate carried over,
  pH 7 would trap more than pH 6; the paper has no pH 6 arm and its buffers change with pH (acetate /
  phosphate / borate; borate complexes ribose) [!].
- **Sugar lever.** Ribose consumes more lysine than SP at 80–120 °C (56 % vs 44 % at 80 °C). In P3 that
  means a reactive sugar competes with lipid carbonyls for the same amines. Glucose is not tested. With
  amines in large excess in P3, this competition is unlikely to change aldehyde trapping much (one
  sentence of reasoning, not a measurement).
- **Sulfur lever.** Not tested.
- **Temperature.** 24 h at 80–120 °C vs 20 min at 140 °C in P3; 24 h is roughly 70× longer, so the
  extents here are upper bounds on what P3 reaches by this route alone.
- **Use:** a structure pointer for a pH-dependent amine sink and the sign of its pH response (stronger
  near neutral pH). **Not usable for a declared band.**
- **Same-lab note [!].** Seville group; one fold with `alaiz1999`, `zamora2005`, `hidalgo2006` and the
  zamora/hidalgo series under a leave-one-laboratory-out split. Not a hold-out.

## 6. FLAGS

1. **Duplicate preparations, means only.** No SD printed for any amino acid value.
2. **pH confounded with buffer species** (acetate 4.0, phosphate 7.4, borate 10.0). Borate binds
   ribose cis-diols and may suppress ribose reactivity at pH 10.
3. **SP dose is nominal** (MW 200 assumed for a complex mixture); its aldehyde content is not given.
4. **Carbonyl excess** (§4): not a model of aldehyde removal by protein.
5. **"pH 7" is phosphate pH 7.4.**
