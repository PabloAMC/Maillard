# **Absolute Quantification of Protein Damage and Maillard Reaction Markers in the Thermomechanical Extrusion of Pea and Soy Isolates**

## **1\. Introduction to the Thermomechanical Dynamics of Plant Proteins in Extrusion Systems**

Contemporary food engineering has positioned thermomechanical extrusion, both in its low-moisture (LME, typically ![][image1] 40%) and high-moisture (HME, \> 50%) variants, as the fundamental technological platform for the transformation of globular plant proteins into structured biopolymers. Soy protein isolate (SPI) and pea protein isolate (PPI) dominate this industrial landscape due to their relatively balanced amino acid profiles and inherent techno-functional properties. SPI is predominantly composed of 11S (glycinin) and 7S (![][image2]\-conglycinin) globulins, while PPI is characterized by homologous fractions rich in legumin (11S) and vicilin/convicilin (7S). In their native state, these proteins exhibit a compact globular structure, stabilized by intricate networks of hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and, critically, internal disulfide bonds.  
The extrusion process operates as a non-equilibrium thermodynamic reactor. As the protein biomass advances through the screw profiles (whether in single or twin-screw configurations), it is subjected to an environment of extreme stress routinely characterized by barrel temperatures ranging between 120°C and 200°C, massive hydrodynamic pressures, and intense shear forces. This combination is macroscopically quantified through Specific Mechanical Energy (SME), a parameter that integrates motor torque, angular screw speed, and mass flow rate to represent the exact amount of mechanical work transferred to the polymer matrix.  
Under these plasticization conditions, thermal energy and SME overcome the entropic barrier of globular proteins, forcing a massive unfolding (denaturation) of tertiary and quaternary structures. Hydrophobic amino acid residues and reactive groups that were previously buried in the protein core become exposed to the aqueous interface. This transition state allows the realignment of polypeptide chains in the cooling die, where laminar forces dictate the formation of an anisotropic fibrous architecture, mimicking the texture of skeletal muscle tissue.  
However, the physicochemical violence required to induce this polymer phase transition inevitably triggers a cascade of secondary chemical reactions of profound nutritional and toxicological relevance. Nucleophilic amino acid residues, particularly lysine, become hyper-reactive substrates. Simultaneously, the presence of residual carbohydrates (even in high-purity isolates) and the thermal degradation of trace lipids provide the electrophilic dicarbonyl precursors necessary to initiate non-enzymatic glycosylation, universally known as the Maillard reaction.  
This report performs a systematic and comprehensive dissection of peer-reviewed literature (2010–2026), focusing on the absolute quantification of these reactive phenomena. The research delineates the thermokinetic rates of reactive lysine loss, the quantitative formation of early and advanced thermal damage markers (furosine, lysinoalanine, carboxymethyllysine, and carboxyethyllysine), the mechanistic evolution of sulfhydryl groups toward covalent cross-linking (disulfide bonds versus isopeptide bonds), and the paradoxical kinetic trajectories of acrylamide as a function of residence time, moisture, and the thermal profile in the extruder barrel.

## **2\. Dynamics of Absolute Reactive Lysine Loss under Thermomechanical Stress**

Lysine is consistently the most vulnerable essential amino acid during the thermal processing of food matrices. Its vulnerability lies in its molecular structure: a long aliphatic side chain culminating in a highly nucleophilic ![][image3]\-amino group. During SME-induced unfolding in the extruder's melting zone, this ![][image3]\-amino group is sterically free to react via nucleophilic addition with the carbonyl group of reducing sugars, forming an unstable Schiff base that rapidly undergoes rearrangement to form an Amadori product (fructosyl-lysine or lactulosyl-lysine, depending on the source sugar). This covalent modification functionally blocks lysine, making it inaccessible for enzymatic cleavage in the gastrointestinal tract (pepsin, trypsin, pancreatin) and reducing the biological value of the meat analogue.

### **2.1. Analytical Methodologies for Reactive Lysine Quantification**

To discriminate between total lysine (conventionally quantified after severe 6 N HCl acid hydrolysis, which reverses some early Maillard phases) and physiologically available lysine, analytical chemistry employs specific derivatizing reagents that bind exclusively to unmodified free primary amino groups.  
The o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) and 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (FDNB, known as Sanger's reagent) represent the gold standards for this quantification. The FDNB reagent reacts with intact reactive lysine to form dinitrophenyl-lysine (DNP-lysine), a chromophore complex that, following subsequent acid hydrolysis to destroy unconjugated lysine (that already blocked by Maillard), allows the exact determination by spectrophotometry or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) of the amount of lysine that survived the extrusion process. Concurrently, the OPA method, coupled with fluorescence detection, provides a rapid measurement of free ![][image4] and ![][image3]\-amino groups, being highly sensitive to reductions in bioavailability.

### **2.2. Quantitative Loss Rates as a Function of PPI and SPI**

A comparative analysis between soy protein and pea protein isolates exposes thermodynamic differences in their resistance to damage. Kinetic studies demonstrate that, under mild to moderate extrusion conditions, lysine depletion (calculated as a percentage of the raw material content) remains at a basal range of approximately 11% according to the FDNB reactive lysine assay. However, upon entering the severe texturization regime required to emulate muscle fibrosity (high temperatures and high SME), the loss of reactive lysine accelerates exponentially.  
Absolute evaluations based on protein nitrogen illustrate the magnitude of the damage. During the comprehensive processing of soy isolate-based meat analogues (MaSoy)—which involves intensive thermomechanical shearing and sterilization—total amino acid content decreases by an alarming average of 30.96 ± 1.86%.1 In absolute terms, the lysine level in SPI drops vertically from a native state of 4.05 ± 0.05 g/16 g N down to 2.78 ± 0.24 g/16 g N.1 This loss of over 30% of the essential amino acid represents a critical nutritional depletion that demands exogenous fortification in functional food formulations.  
In stark contrast, pea protein isolate (MaPea) exhibits greater resilience against the same thermomechanical history. Overall nitrogen-based amino acid reductions in PPI matrices are limited to 14.36 ± 2.76% 1, and lysine experiences significantly less deterioration, dropping from 4.43 ± 0.19 to 3.75 ± 0.04 g/16 g N.1 This reactivity differential can be attributed to the lower propensity of pea vicilin globular subunits to undergo irreversible unfolding compared to soy ![][image2]\-conglycinin under identical specific mechanical energy levels.

### **2.3. The Thermodynamic Triad: Barrel Temperature, SME, and Moisture**

The retention of reactive lysine is governed by an intricate balance among the three master variables of the extruder: barrel zone temperature, hydrodynamic friction (SME), and in-barrel moisture content.  
The barrel temperature, which routinely oscillates between 120°C and 200°C during texturization, acts as the primary thermodynamic catalyst. The literature reveals that the degradation of free amino groups follows pseudo-order kinetics that accelerate markedly after crossing the 140°C threshold. At 120°C, heat is invested primarily in protein unfolding (denaturation enthalpy). However, upon reaching 160°C – 180°C in the melting zone, thermal energy comfortably surpasses the activation energy required for Schiff bases to irreversibly rearrange and degrade into advanced Maillard compounds, obliterating reactive lysine. The correlation between barrel temperature and lysine retention is, therefore, inversely proportional and exponential in nature.  
Moisture content acts as a powerful plasticizing agent that indirectly modulates chemical degradation. An increase in system moisture promotes a net positive retention of reactive lysine. In high-moisture extrusion (HME, \> 50%) regimes, subcritical water reduces the apparent viscosity of the protein melt. This drop in viscosity minimizes internal friction and viscous dissipation, translating directly into a reduction of the Specific Mechanical Energy (SME) exerted by the co-rotating screws. For context, varying screw speeds from 350 to 550 rpm at temperatures of 180°C to 190°C can generate SME values between 13.71 and 21.72 kJ/kg, though other extruder setups report SME increasing from 111 to 133 kcal/kg (approx. 464 to 556 kJ/kg) as screw speeds increase from 250 to 450 rpm. By decreasing SME, extreme mechanical tearing of polypeptide chains is mitigated, preserving partial steric integrity of globulins and reducing the exposure of lysine-rich internal domains. Additionally, according to the law of mass action, an exceptionally high water volume dilutes the effective concentration of reducing sugars and amino residues, depressing the rate of molecular collisions required to initiate Maillard condensation.  
Conversely, under low-moisture (LME) regimes combined with high screw RPM (which spikes SME), the biomass acts as an ultra-dense viscoelastic fluid. Dissipated frictional heat causes sudden local melt temperature spikes (frequently exceeding the temperature programmed in the barrel jackets), drastically accelerating the absolute consumption of free lysine.

## **3\. Quantitative Formation of Lysinoalanine and Maillard Markers (Furosine, CML, CEL)**

The disappearance of reactive lysine is not a thermodynamic black hole; molecular mass is conserved through the formation of blocked analogues and intermediate compounds of varying toxicity. The quantification of these neo-compounds requires high-sensitivity chromatographic platforms, predominantly tandem mass spectrometry coupled to ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC-MS/MS), utilizing internal isotopic standards (such as CML-d4 and CEL-d4) to achieve absolute precisions in mg/kg.

### **3.1. Furosine Kinetics as an Early-Stage Indicator**

Furosine (![][image3]\-N-(2-furoylmethyl)-L-lysine) is an artificial, non-biologically present amino acid that is generated *ex vivo* exclusively following laboratory acid hydrolysis of proteins containing stable Amadori intermediates (such as fructosyl-lysine). Consequently, furosine content serves as the definitive surrogate marker to audit incipient thermal damage in extruders.  
In legume texturization, furosine exhibits accumulation and degradation kinetics highly dependent on residence time (RTD). Even under moderate SME and temperature parameters (e.g., 140°C), the formation of the Amadori compound in plant isolates reaches its molecular zenith almost instantaneously. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that, in extruded blends rich in legume proteins homologous to soy and pea, furosine concentrations of approximately 8.7 mg per 100 g of protein are quantified 2, evidencing unavoidable Maillard damage intrinsic to thermomechanical friction. Additionally, intermediate markers like hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) have been measured at 11.34 μg/g at 140°C extrusion temperatures.1 In comparative studies, extruded pet foods exhibited fructoselysine/furosine levels averaging 4534 mg/kg dry matter, highlighting the severity of the Maillard reaction under these conditions.1  
However, the use of furosine as a sole marker in severe extrusion (160°C–200°C) presents a critical methodological flaw. The life curve of Amadori products is not asymptotic; after reaching a maximum concentration, fructosyl-lysine compounds become thermodynamically unstable and undergo oxidative cleavage, dehydration, and fragmentation to generate free ![][image4]\-dicarbonyl compounds. At temperatures above 150°C in the extruder barrel, the degradation rate of the Amadori compound far exceeds its formation rate, causing furosine levels to drop sharply even though overall protein damage has worsened. Therefore, it is imperative to complement this measurement with the quantification of Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs).

### **3.2. Massive Formation of Carboxymethyllysine (CML) and Carboxyethyllysine (CEL)**

AGEs are non-degradable, chemically inert compounds in biological systems, whose exogenous accumulation through diet promotes tissue oxidative stress and systemic pathologies. The main target species in extruded plant proteins are N\-![][image3]\-(carboxymethyl)lysine (CML) and N\-![][image3]\-(carboxyethyl)lysine (CEL).  
The mechanistic synthesis of these two markers differs substantially. CML is formed by the direct oxidative cleavage of pre-existing Amadori products or through the reaction of the free ![][image3]\-amino group of lysine with *glyoxal* (GO), a two-carbon dicarbonyl fragment derived both from lipid oxidation and sugar autoxidation. CEL, on the other hand, originates via an analogous route where the electrophilic reactant is *methylglyoxal* (MGO), a highly reactive three-carbon molecule derived preferentially from the retro-aldol condensation of sugars and glycolytic metabolism.  
Contemporary chromatographic investigations of extruded plant isolates and commercial meat analogues reveal alarming concentrations of both compounds. UHPLC-MS/MS data establish an absolute range for CML between 16.0 and 110.0 mg/kg 4, with experimental average values in isolated models of 16.46 to 47.61 mg/kg.5 Simultaneously, CEL proves to be the dominant AGE in these thermal processes, exhibiting massive ranges from 25.0 to 110.0 mg/kg 4, and mean clusters between 25.21 and 86.23 mg/kg.5 For perspective, raw pork contains significantly lower baseline levels of these advanced glycation end products, specifically 32.52 mg/kg of CML and 10.70 mg/kg of CEL (expressed per kg of protein).6  
The most revealing kinetic finding regarding AGE formation lies in the thermodynamic divergence of CML and CEL against extruder parameters (Table 1).

The thermodynamic paradox dictates that extrusion at temperatures exceeding 130°C surprisingly inhibits the formation of CML and glyoxal, while exponentially exacerbating the synthesis of CEL, methylglyoxal, and 2,3-butanedione. The activation energy for methylglyoxal generation is optimally reached in the 121°C–140°C regime, and given that the rate constant (![][image8]) for the MGO-lysine reaction is intrinsically higher than that of GO, CEL accumulates preferentially at high temperatures.  
In parallel, barrel hydration controls a phase bifurcation. Increasing the water content injected into the extruder strongly represses CEL formation, acting as a heat sink that curbs the dehydration pathways leading to MGO. Paradoxically, increasing moisture accelerates CML accumulation, since the aqueous environment fosters the polar solvation necessary for glyoxal oxidative fragmentation reactions. Consequently, it is thermodynamically impossible to simultaneously minimize CML and CEL by altering a single extruder variable; control requires non-linear temperature and fluid injection profiles.

### **3.3. Toxic Cross-linking: Quantitative Formation of Lysinoalanine (LAL)**

Lysinoalanine (N\-![][image3]\-(DL-2-amino-2-carboxyethyl)-L-lysine) constitutes a collateral damage marker independent of reducing sugars. It is an intramolecular or intermolecular covalent cross-link generated when the ![][image3]\-amino group of a lysine residue undergoes nucleophilic addition to the highly reactive double bond of a dehydroalanine residue. The precursor dehydroalanine is produced through the ![][image2]\-elimination of cysteine, serine, or phosphoserine residues, reactions strongly catalyzed by an increase in pH (alkaline conditions) or prolonged thermal stress.  
The quantification of LAL in SPI and PPI processing introduces traceability complexity. Analyses reveal that very short-duration thermomechanical shearing in the extruder alone does not always generate massive *de novo* LAL concentrations. However, the genesis of the problem precedes extrusion. The vast majority of commercial soy and pea isolates are manufactured via alkaline extraction and isoelectric precipitation, a process involving the solvation of flour at a pH of 8.0 to 10.0. This initial alkaline exposure, combined with spray drying, induces a pre-existing degree of LAL formation in the raw material before it enters the extruder barrel.  
The extruder acts as a conditional amplifier. If extrusion is performed at low moisture regimes (allowing sudden frictional temperature spikes above 160°C), thermal energy breaks native disulfide bonds (-S-S-), releasing thiols that undergo ![][image2]\-elimination toward dehydroalanine. In final commercial formulations, it is the conjunction of the extrusion thermal history followed by a lethal thermal treatment (autoclave sterilization at 125.5°C for extended periods of up to 26 minutes for shelf-stable products) that detonates lysinoalanine levels, making LAL a significant indigestibility factor, as the bond cannot be hydrolyzed by mammalian digestive proteases. True ileal digestibility decreases in direct proportion to the increase in absolute post-extrusion LAL concentrations (mg/kg).

## **4\. High-Energy Covalent Cross-linking and Post-Extrusion Sulfhydryl Group Modulation**

The metamorphosis from an amorphous globular powder to highly organized fibrous structures is the triumph of extrusion science. This "texturization" is a phenomenon primarily mediated by intermolecular forces, where the type and degree of covalent cross-linking dictate the macroscopic rheology and structural integrity against mastication processes. There are two predominant paradigms of covalent cross-linking in extruded plant protein literature: the oxidation of sulfhydryls to form disulfide bonds and the isopeptide synthesis of ![][image3]\-(![][image9]\-glutamyl)lysine bonds.

### **4.1. The Thermodynamic Dominance of Disulfide Bonds (-S-S-)**

Systematic evidence based on Raman spectroscopy, FTIR, and electrophoretic fractionation under reductive versus non-reductive conditions (with ![][image2]\-mercaptoethanol or DTT) unequivocally concludes that intermolecular disulfide bonds operate as the primary architectural pillar of thermally formed soy and pea protein networks.  
The atomic-level mechanics correlate with the dissipation of Specific Mechanical Energy (SME). Plant globulins (particularly the 11S fraction, such as legumin and glycinin) possess rich cysteine domains. In their native or pre-extruded state, the vast majority of these residues are internally stabilized by intramolecular disulfide bonds that fold the subunit, or exist as thiol groups (-SH) sterically hidden in extremely hydrophobic cores.  
Advancing through the compression and melting chambers of the twin-screw extruder, the biomass simultaneously experiences temperatures up to 160°C–180°C and high tangential forces. This thermomechanical violence breaks non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces) and partially cleaves pre-existing intramolecular disulfide bonds. This phenomenon causes a massive unfolding and exteriorization of free sulfhydryl groups, rendering them temporarily hyper-reactive.  
As the polymer melt enters the directional zone of the cooling die, laminar flow aligns the stretched polypeptide chains. In this densely packed and thermo-oxidative environment, massive thiol-disulfide interchange and direct oxidation reactions occur, covalently cross-linking the different chains. Analytical quantification of free \-SH group content using colorimetric reagents like Ellman's reagent (DTNB) demonstrates an absolute and drastic decrease in detectable thiol groups post-extrusion. This \-SH drop does not represent a loss of matter, but the stoichiometric confirmation that free thiols have condensed oxidatively to form the new network of densely cross-linked intermolecular disulfide bonds that impart toughness to the final extrudate.

### **4.2. The Insignificance of Spontaneous Thermal Isopeptidation**

In contrast to disulfide bonds, ![][image3]\-(![][image9]\-glutamyl)lysine isopeptide bonds generate extremely strong intermolecular links between amino acid side chains, directly binding the ![][image9]\-carboxamide group of glutamine to the ![][image3]\-amino group of lysine.  
Despite their remarkable resistance to both massive thermomechanical stress and cleavage by gastric and intestinal peptidases, analytical data based on mass spectrometry confirm that the purely thermal spontaneous formation of intramolecular or intermolecular isopeptide bonds during standard PPI and SPI extrusion is kinetically residual or outright insignificant. The activation barrier for condensing secondary amides from these side chains without enzymatic catalysis demands prolonged steric proximity and localized dehydration that the short residence time of the extruder (tens of seconds) cannot sustain.  
Consequently, scientific literature concludes that ![][image3]\-(![][image9]\-glutamyl)lysine bonds acquire quantitative relevance in extrusion *only* if the pre-conditioned material has been enzymatically modified through the exogenous incorporation of microbial transglutaminase (TGase or mTGase, E.C. 2.3.2.13).7 Experimental applications of MTGase typically range from 0.1 to 0.6 units/g 8, and it has been shown that achieving an ![][image3]\-(![][image9]\-glutamyl)lysine content of just 3 μmol/100g (using approximately 0.03% MTGase) is sufficient to drastically improve the macroscopic tensile properties of the resulting gels.9 Treatment with mTGase actively catalyzes acyl transfer between glutamine and lysine in the cold or at moderate incubation temperatures, resulting in extrudates with astronomically superior macroscopic tensile and viscoelastic strengths. High-resolution nuclear magnetic resonance (![][image10]H-NMR) confirms that the spectral peaks associated with this isopeptide are virtually exclusive to biocatalyzed batches. If mTGase is implemented in the formulation, the collateral negative impact again falls on protein digestibility by drastically reducing the volume of physiologically available reactive lysine.

## **5\. Kinetic Curves and Specific Acrylamide Formation Mechanisms**

Acrylamide is an aliphatic diamide, toxicologically classified as genotoxic and probably carcinogenic to humans, that emerges as a hidden and prevalent risk in the design of novel food systems. Like carboxyethyllysine (CEL), its synthesis obeys primarily the advanced phase of the Maillard cascade, generated through the aldol condensation and subsequent decarboxylation between the free ![][image4]\-amino group of an asparagine residue and a carbonyl functional group originating from dicarbonyl intermediates, which are intrinsically derived from reducing sugars and starches (frequently co-extruded along with SPI and PPI to optimize expansion or viscosity).  
The hyper-thermomechanical environment of the extruder acts as a flash-cooking and high frictional dehydration setting that provides a prime kinetic environment to propel initial asparagine condensation. Analytical databases obtained across parametric configurations reveal counterintuitive interactions surrounding acrylamide synthesis.

### **5.1. Static Dynamic Quantification and Baseline**

Static analyses derived from UPLC-QqQ-MS/MS chromatography coupled to electrospray ionization (ESI+) document measurable and elevated concentrations of acrylamide in virtually all commercial and experimental variants of extruded meat analogues. Direct kinetic monitoring in soy matrices demonstrated that the temporal progression in the extruder barrel at a temperature of 130°C with an extremely ephemeral residence time, between 20 to 30 seconds, induced a sharp jump in basal acrylamide, going from 22.36 µg/kg in the raw flour to 62.62 µg/kg almost immediately.3  
In recent cross-sectional studies evaluating a multitude of over-the-counter plant-based meat analogues formulated from varying concentrations of SPI and PPI, cumulative acrylamide amounts expand dramatically. Quantifications in the post-processed product systematically range between 31.81 µg/kg and 186.70 µg/kg (equivalent to 0.032 \- 0.190 mg/kg).5 It is vital to underscore that in numerous experimental extrusion profiles, the upper margins substantially exceed the institutional warning threshold typically set in Europe for analogue cereals and baked goods (0.065 mg/kg).  
Additionally, a physical mechanism has been elucidated whereby newly formed acrylamide reversibly interacts with the surrounding biomass. In vitro evaluations (fluorescence and simulated digestion) confirmed that native SPI is capable of trapping or sterically binding to free acrylamide molecules via covalent and van der Waals bonds. This structural interaction exerts a static quenching effect on the intrinsic fluorescence of the soy protein isolate, severely delaying the release of acrylamide in gastrointestinal tract simulators and complicating final toxicological bioavailability estimations.

### **5.2. Thermodynamic Kinetics: The Extrusion Paradox**

Acrylamide kinetic curves do not obey a unified linear vector but behave as multivariate responses paradoxically linked to moisture content and extreme heating.

1. **Moisture Phase Inversion Paradox**: The influence of total system moisture on the acrylamide synthesis rate exhibits a bifurcated behavior depending on the global regime. In Low-Moisture Extrusion (LME, ![][image1] 40%), typical of dry textured vegetable protein (TVP) formulations, an incremental increase in moisture positively promotes acrylamide generation. In this quasi-solid state, the addition of water molecules provides plastic mobilization, facilitating microscopic collisions of asparagine with electrophilic substrates without drastically altering screw friction. Conversely, transitioning toward current technical vanguard, High-Moisture Extrusion (HME, ![][image11] 50%), abundant water forcefully inhibits the toxin's formation. Hydration above 50% suppresses frictional dissipation, acts as an immense heat sink, and alters the specific heat of the thermoplastic mass, energetically drowning the dehydration and pyrolytic decarboxylation needed to stabilize the final acrylamide molecule.  
2. **Thermal Dichotomy at Extreme Temperatures**: The temperature variable in the cooking zone introduces a profound kinetic detour. Raising the temperature from low ranges (e.g., 100°C) toward 130°C–140°C exponentially increases formation curves following traditional Maillard dynamics. However, as the barrel temperature scales to severe regimes above 150°C–170°C, an inflection point occurs where the absolute accumulation of measurable acrylamide paradoxically begins to decrease. This anomalous decline in the kinetic constant (![][image8]) is grounded in a principle of aggressive molecular competition. At very high temperatures, dicarbonyl intermediates, such as acetaldehydes and hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), are sequestered via Pictet-Spengler condensation with free tryptophan amino acid residues, directing the molar mass toward the explosive formation of polycyclic heterocyclic amines (HAA), with harman being the predominant species. This competitive pathway depletes the chemical reservoir and effectively stalls the acrylamide metabolic axis, while also promoting volatilization in the late phases of the extruder.  
3. **Residence Time (RTD) and Screw Implications**: The time protein and sugars interact under maximum stress defines the final limit of the reaction. Although altering the geometric configuration or speed (RPM) of the twin screws slightly modulates shear, its dominant effect is on the temporal parameter. Significant increases in screw speed can statistically decrease acrylamide content by forcing early ejection of the biomass, decreasing the mean thermodynamic residence time so that Schiff base formation kinetics freeze before final pyrolysis. Consequently, higher mass feed rates associated with shorter barrels and accelerated screws confer kinetic windows too ephemeral for the toxin to escalate to concerning levels.

## **6\. Conclusion and Synthesis of Chemical-Nutritional Implications**

The global implementation of extrusion technologies (LME and HME) for the conversion of native protein isolates (SPI and PPI) into muscle tissue analogues has proven to be a fundamental achievement in texture bioengineering. As comprehensively illustrated by this review, the underlying thermomechanical architecture enabling fibrillar anisotropy rests on the forced exploitation of molecular unfolding: a pronounced drop in free sulfhydryl residues, directionally oxidized into a high-energy disulfide bond super-network, virtually dispensing with covalent isopeptide modulation, except in enzymatically biocatalyzed profiles.  
However, this macrostructural plasticization exhibits a colossal thermodynamic cost at the molecular and nutritional levels. Absolute damage quotas reveal depletions routinely exceeding 30% in native reactive lysine levels. The extruded plant protein matrix invariably evolves into a Maillard Reaction bioreactor; initiating with rapid transient increases in Amadori markers like furosine, culminating in the accumulation of irreducible, xenobiotic, and toxic chemical species. Concentrations detected by UHPLC-MS/MS place astonishingly high ranges for AGEs such as carboxymethyllysine (CML, driven by hydration) and carboxyethyllysine (CEL, exacerbated by temperatures above 130°C), frequently surpassing a combined 110 mg/kg in commercial analogues.  
Simultaneously, the emergence of lysinoalanine and the unpredictable kinetics of acrylamide—the latter thermodynamically sequestered at extreme temperatures by competitive routes producing harman—delineate an analytical scenario that challenges the classical extrusion optimization paradigm. In summary, future modeling must transcend a purely rheological approach and adopt unified phenomenological equations. Only through the infinitesimal balancing of supercritical moisture, fractionated temperature control in the barrel, and parametric suppression of residence time (RTD) will it be possible to design structured protein foods that possess not only biomechanical fidelity to animal fiber but also retain unaltered bioavailability profiles free from oxidative glycation molecules that limit long-term health.
