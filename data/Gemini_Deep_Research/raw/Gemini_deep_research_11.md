# **Simultaneous Quantification and Thermodynamic Dynamics of Maillard Volatiles and Lipid Oxidation Interactions in Intact Soy and Pea Protein Matrices**

## **1\. The Paradigm of Absolute Quantification in the Protein Matrix**

Flavor architecture in plant-based meat analogs represents one of the most intricate physicochemical challenges in contemporary food science. The goal of replicating the volatile profile of animal meat requires the suppression of intrinsic undesirable notes (herbaceous and "beany" off-notes) and the simultaneous generation of a complex "meaty" aromatic spectrum through the addition of exogenous precursors. However, the vast majority of published literature evaluates these interactions using relative peak areas (percentages) obtained via Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS). This analytical approach is fundamentally flawed for flavor thermodynamics, as it ignores the differential response coefficients of the mass spectrometer and, critically, obscures the true magnitude of the phase partitioning of volatile compounds.1

The headspace above an intact Soy Protein Isolate (SPI) or Pea Protein Isolate (PPI) matrix is not an ideal system in equilibrium. High-molecular-weight globular proteins—such as glycinin (11S) and ![][image1]\-conglycinin (7S) in soy, and legumin (11S) and vicilin (7S) in pea—act as active "chemical sponges".2 These macromolecules modulate the release of odorants into the gas phase through a complex network of steric entrapment, non-covalent binding, and irreversible covalent bonds.4 Therefore, to accurately model flavor release kinetics and molecular antagonism, the simultaneous reporting of absolute concentrations (![][image2]g/kg or ppb) calculated through rigorous calibration curves using internal standards (such as E-2-octenal, 2,4,6-trimethylpyridine, or isotopically labeled analogs) within the GC-MS system is strictly necessary.6

Exhaustive investigation of studies published between 2015 and 2026 meeting this analytical rigor reveals that Maillard reaction pathways (induced by exogenous precursors like cysteine, ribose, and xylose) and the lipid peroxidation pathway (generating compounds like hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol) do not operate in isolation.9 Conversely, when evaluated simultaneously within an aqueous suspension or a thermomechanical intact plant protein system, they exhibit profound competitive cross-talk. The progress of the Maillard reaction not only sensorially masks the lipid off-notes defect but also thermodynamically and kinetically interferes with the synthesis, partitioning, and release of volatile aldehydes like hexanal, permanently modifying the headspace composition.10

## **2\. Baseline Profile: Absolute Quantification of Lipid Off-Notes in Intact Matrices**

To understand the magnitude of competition for protein binding sites, it is imperative to establish the absolute baseline concentrations of primary flavor defects in SPI and PPI. The sensory identity of legume isolates is dominated by residual lipid degradation products. Despite commercial defatting processes, a fraction of polyunsaturated lipids (predominantly linoleic acid and ![][image3]\-linolenic acid) remains tightly bound to the hydrophobic core of 11S globulins.12

During hydration and initial thermal processing, these fatty acids undergo homolytic cleavage and subsequent ![][image1]\-scission. This process is initially catalyzed by residual or pre-existing lipoxygenase (LOX) enzymes and later driven by thermal autoxidation.10 Linoleic acid degradation predominantly yields hexanal, a highly volatile six-carbon aldehyde that constitutes the universal chemical marker for herbaceous, green, and "beany" flavor in plant proteins.16 In parallel, ![][image3]\-linolenic acid generates unsaturated aldehydes and alkyl furans like 2-pentylfuran, while other degradation pathways produce 1-octen-3-ol, responsible for mushroom and earthy notes.17

Absolute quantification measurements using Headspace Solid-Phase Microextraction (HS-SPME) reveal massive loads of these compounds before any Maillard intervention. In untreated pea protein systems, hexanal levels up to 1260 ![][image2]g/kg have been documented, generating an overwhelming Odor Activity Value (OAV) of 280, given that the human detection threshold for hexanal in water is extremely low (approximately 4.5 to 5.0 ![][image2]g/kg).16 3-methylbutanoic acid (derived from amino acid degradation) is also present in extreme absolute concentrations of 4580 ![][image4] 471 ![][image2]g/kg in raw peas.1616

In soy protein isolates, the absolute abundance of saturated and unsaturated aldehydes overwhelmingly dominates the headspace. An instrumental quantification study (2026) measured hexanal concentration in soy-based texturization control matrices at 605.64 ![][image4] 6.50 ![][image2]g/kg, accompanied by nonanal at 74.37 ![][image4] 0.11 ![][image2]g/kg.10 These molecules not only present a primary organoleptic defect but, due to their electrophilic carbonyl structure, act as formidable reactants. Free hexanal present in the aqueous suspension will rapidly saturate nucleophilic sites on the intact protein before the exogenously driven Maillard reaction has a chance to fully develop.20

Any reactive aldehyde has a massive stoichiometric advantage in occupying the covalent binding sites of the intact protein.

## **3\. Thermochemical Engineering: Insertion of Exogenous Precursors and Maillard Dynamics**

To overcome the hexanal obstacle and generate the desired "meaty" profile (meaty, roasty, brothy), exogenous flavor precursors are introduced into the aqueous suspension matrix of SPI or PPI prior to thermal treatment (boiling, autoclaving, or extrusion). The most efficient and rigorously quantified experimental formulas utilize fast-reacting simple reducing sugars (aldopentoses like D-xylose or ribose) in combination with free sulfur-donating amino acids (cysteine or methionine), glutathione, and occasionally, precursor vitamins like thiamine.11

The progress of the Maillard reaction in this environment is a cascade highly sensitive to pH, temperature, and water activity (![][image5]). Initially, the ![][image3]\-amino group of cysteine or exposed residues of the soy protein attacks the carbonyl group of ribose or xylose, forming an unstable glycosylamine that rapidly rearranges through the Amadori rearrangement to form ketosamines (1-amino-1-deoxy-2-ketoses).23 The subsequent enolization and dehydration of Amadori compounds generate reactive diketones (dicarbonyls) such as 1-deoxyosone and 3-deoxyosone.

It is in this intermediate phase where the presence of exogenous cysteine dictates the pathway's diversion toward the synthesis of desirable aromas. Through Strecker degradation, dicarbonyl compounds react with cysteine, causing its decarboxylation and oxidative deamination. This pathway cleaves cysteine, releasing hydrogen sulfide (![][image6]), ammonia (![][image7]), and acetaldehyde.24 The thermal polymerization of these low-molecular-weight intermediates with furans derived from sugar dehydration is the originating source of heterocycles containing sulfur, nitrogen, and oxygen.

When using Maillard reaction systems to mask off-flavors and generate meaty aromas, the exact dosage of sulfhydryls is critical. Recent quantitative models have established that adding cysteine in the specific range of 0.25% to 0.75% optimally increases meaty, caramel, and umami notes while simultaneously decreasing the bitter taste of the reaction products. 11

### **3.1 Absolute Quantification of Meaty Odorants**

Simultaneous headspace quantification of these reactive mixtures has confirmed the absolute generation of potent odorants with superlative OAV values:

* **2-methyl-3-furanthiol (MFT):** The absolute pillar of beef broth and roasted chicken flavor. This monocyclic compound features a highly reactive thiol group and an almost infinitesimal olfactometric odor threshold (0.005 to 0.01 ![][image2]g/kg).21 Its formation yield from the cysteine-ribose system is directly proportional to the extrusion or heating temperature (typically \>120 °C).26  
* **2-furfurylthiol (FFT):** Primary derivative of the reaction of furfural (pentose degradation product) with hydrogen sulfide generated by cysteine. It imparts roasted coffee and roasted meat notes.22  
* **Methional \[3-(methylthio)propanal\]:** Direct product of Strecker degradation of methionine, with an intense odor of boiled potato and meat extract.25  
* **Pyrazines (e.g., 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine):** Formed by the condensation of Strecker aminoketones. They provide the critical roasted, bread crust, and nutty notes.16 The olfactory threshold of 2,5-dimethylpyrazine is low (1.75 ![][image2]g/kg), making it a fundamental marker of high-temperature Maillard progress.30

Under ideal conditions, the addition of sulfhydryls ensures an expansive meaty profile. However, the introduction of these precursors into intact globular plant protein matrices yields headspace recoveries far below the simple aqueous models predicted by theory. This is not due to a synthesis failure, but because Maillard products are sequestered by the matrix itself, and simultaneously, the Maillard reaction exerts a direct suppressive effect on lipid degradation pathways, profoundly altering the simultaneous concentrations of herbaceous defects.10

## **4\. Quantitative Suppression and Kinetic Modification of Hexanal**

One of the primary directives in plant-based protein science is to understand whether the addition of flavor precursors and the consequent progress of the Maillard reaction alter the presence of free hexanal. Scrutiny of GC-MS instrumental data (2015-2026) employing simultaneous quantification in ![][image2]g/kg conclusively reveals that the progress of the Maillard reaction and the deliberate addition of sulfhydryls (cysteine) severely suppress and kinetically modify the release of hexanal into the headspace.10

This reduction is not simply due to psycho-physical masking in the human olfactory bulb (known as *mixture suppression*, where potent furan odors drown out hexanal in the brain), but is a true chemical suppression at the molecular level within the protein matrix.11

A benchmark study on High Moisture Extrusion (HME) of meat analogs demonstrated that by modulating matrix parameters to promote thermochemical reactions, free hexanal is systematically reduced. Specifically, a control matrix (0 minutes of enzymatic pretreatment) contained 605.64 ![][image4] 6.50 ![][image2]g/kg of hexanal. This absolute concentration dropped to 478.12 ![][image4] 6.39 ![][image2]g/kg after 40 minutes of pretreatment, and further decreased to 430.94 ![][image4] 1.88 ![][image2]g/kg at 80 minutes, representing a quantified absolute maximum reduction of 174.7 ![][image2]g/kg in hexanal availability.1010

This kinetic suppression is heavily dependent on precursor availability. It has been quantified that extending enzymatic pretreatment from 0 to 80 minutes before extrusion increases the total free amino acid content in the matrix from 409.27 mg/100 g to 844.69 mg/100 g. This massive release of precursors includes a significant surge in total umami amino acids from 28.87 mg/100 g to 71.16 mg/100 g, providing the necessary stoichiometric fuel to drive the Maillard reaction forward and quench lipid oxidation pathways. 10

This chemical suppression of the undesirable aldehyde through Maillard pathways is governed by three fundamental physicochemical mechanisms:

### **4.1 Direct Radical Quenching by MRPs and Cysteine (In situ Antioxidant Activity)**

The generation of hexanal, nonanal, and 1-octen-3-ol is a dynamic process of free radical propagation in the lipid fraction. When intact SPI and PPI matrices are subjected to exogenous precursors and heat, reducing sugars and amines begin to generate Maillard Reaction Products (MRPs), specifically reductones, high-molecular-weight melanoidins, and polymeric heterocyclic compounds.6

These MRPs act as powerful free radical scavengers in the aqueous matrix. Reductones derived from sugar enolization rapidly donate electrons to intermediate lipid radicals (![][image8], ![][image9]), truncating the homolytic cleavage of the linoleic acid peroxide chain before hexanal can physically cleave from the hydrocarbon chain.34

More specifically, the deliberate addition of cysteine critically exacerbates this antioxidant effect. Cysteine not only reacts with ribose or xylose, but its free thiol group (-SH) possesses an extremely low S-H bond dissociation energy, making it a primary radical interceptor. Real-time headspace quantification reveals a markedly longer lag phase for hexanal formation in systems where cysteine was added prior to heating.6 In aqueous and extrusion experiments, cysteine actively inactivates lipoxygenases (LOX) and halts autoxidation so aggressively that the final absolute concentration of hexanal can fall between 30% and 80% depending on the pH and the initial amino acid load.11 Li et al. and other authors (2026) report a strong negative correlation between the formation of sulfurous Maillard compounds and the presence of hexanal, confirming oxidative neutralization.24

### **4.2 Direct Chemical Interactions (Dicarbonyl Cross-Talk)**

Hexanal silencing is also destructive; that is, the previously formed aldehyde is consumed in the reactive "soup". In SPI aqueous suspensions, thermal conditions driving the Maillard reaction (120-150 °C) increase the kinetic energy of free hexanal. Given the density of precursors, ![][image10]\-unsaturated aldehydes and saturated lipid-derived aldehydes enter the Maillard reaction network as de facto dicarbonyls, reacting with free amines and residual ammonia generated by the Strecker degradation of cysteine and other amino acids.9

Hexanal condenses through crossed aldol condensation reactions or reacts with the produced ![][image6], transforming linear herbaceous molecules into thiazolines, thiazoles, or long-chain alkylpyrazines. These hybrid lipid-Maillard reactions stoichiometrically consume hexanal from the system, removing it from the off-notes profile and integrating it into compounds that frequently lack sufficient volatility to severely affect odor, or present drastically higher olfactory thresholds. GC-MS quantification reflects this thermodynamic alteration as a net collapse of C6 to C9 aldehyde signals in the chromatogram.9

### **4.3 Matrix Topology Modulation (Mass Transfer Restriction)**

The third documented suppression pathway relies on system rheology and mass transfer. The addition of precursors, particularly cysteine, alters the three-dimensional structural state of intact proteins in aqueous solutions and HME processes. The excess of exogenous sulfhydryl groups promotes protein unfolding and triggers a flood of disulfide exchange reactions (-S-S-), increasing the density of the gelation network or extrusion cross-linking.6

This drastic increase in glycinin and legumin polymerization creates a severe steric hindrance. A highly cross-linked plant matrix due to cysteine intervention acts as an almost impenetrable diffusional barrier against dissolved oxygen in the aqueous phase, blocking the penetration required to propagate lipid oxidation in hydrophobic pockets. At the same time, the diffusivity of any residual hexanal trapped inside these macro-structures falls exponentially, dramatically reducing its vapor pressure and quantifiable partitioning into the analyzable headspace volume.10

## **5\. Thermodynamics of Odorant Entrapment: Non-Covalent Interactions**

The success or failure of thermochemistry to endow the protein with a sustainable "meaty" aroma depends not only on suppressing off-notes but on the dynamic release of the generated Maillard volatiles. When absolute concentrations of injected or in situ synthesized odorants are simultaneously analyzed against those recovered in the gas phase of intact SPI or PPI, a drastic reduction in their recovery rate (flavor fade) is evident. Plant proteins act as chemical reservoirs through reversible thermodynamic forces.4

### **5.1 Hydrogen Bonds and Hydrophobic Affinity**

Non-covalent entrapment governs the initial partitioning of volatile compounds, allowing a transient equilibrium dictated by thermodynamic partition coefficients. This entrapment is governed by hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonds (H-bonds).

The intact globular 11S and 7S fractions of both pea and soy contain ample hydrophobic domains constituted by apolar residues (leucine, valine, isoleucine, phenylalanine).2 Medium and long-chain aliphatic molecules derived from lipid oxidation, such as hexanal, octanal, nonanal, and 2-pentylfuran, exhibit a high affinity constant for these "pockets". Circular dichroism and fluorescence studies, backed by molecular docking simulations, have mapped how hexanal internalizes into the intact PPI architecture predominantly through multiple coordinated hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl group, and its long aliphatic tail is stabilized by hydrophobic dispersion forces.20

This dynamic has a direct corollary for processing: non-covalent bindings are exothermic in nature, meaning the entrapment of hexanal and related compounds is stronger at low temperatures. As the temperature of SPI aqueous suspensions increases (e.g., pasteurization or hot hydration above 65 °C), the kinetic energy of water molecules and the protein disrupts these thermolabile bindings, causing a flash-release of trapped hexanal into the headspace.42 However, thermal energy simultaneously unfolds the quaternary and tertiary structure of protein globulins, irreversibly exposing new reactive sites that will give way to covalent interactions.

### **5.2 Pi-Pi Stacking (![][image11] Stacking) in Maillard Volatiles**

Unlike aliphatic aldehydes, critical Maillard reaction products for roasted and nutty notes—substituted pyrazines (e.g., 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine)—feature aromatic heterocyclic rings with delocalized electron clouds. Mechanistic studies suggest that pyrazines interact with soy and pea protein isolates primarily through non-covalent mechanisms, specifically via ![][image11] stacking with the benzene rings of tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan residues exposed during thermal protein denaturation.3 Unlike compounds undergoing severe covalent entrapment, ![][image11] stacking compounds maintain an equilibrium partition that ensures a prolonged and relatively stable release of roasty notes in the mouth during mastication of the extruded matrix.

## **6\. Covalent Binding and the Irreversible "Flavor Fade" Phenomenon**

The true barrier to formulating PBMAs with meat profiles lies in covalent interactions.4 This class of binding is chemically irreversible under standard processing and food consumption conditions, resulting in an absolute depletion (flavor fade) of the volatile compound yield in the GC-MS chromatogram. Intact SPI and PPI are densely provided with nucleophilic side chains, especially the ![][image12]\-amino group of lysines, the imidazole ring of histidine, and the free sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of cysteine.

When Maillard reaction precursors and lipid oxidation products are quantified simultaneously, their covalent interactions unfold as a zero-sum competition for these reactive residues.20

### **6.1 Schiff Base Formation**

The electrophilic carbonyl group of hexanal and other saturated aldehydes reacts directly and covalently with the primary amino groups of exposed lysine in soy protein. The nucleophilic attack of nitrogen on the carbonyl carbon generates, through dehydration, a covalent ![][image13] bond (imine), typically termed a Schiff Base.5 Analytical literature corroborates that while free hexanal drastically decreases during warm storage in SPI suspensions, this is fundamentally due to chemisorption via massive imine formation with the protein network, an "armor" matrix that retains the off-flavor, preventing its release to the olfactory receptor, but penalizing the biological value (digestibility) of the essential amino acid lysine.45

### **6.2 Michael Addition and Structural Reactivity**

An additional level of complexity in covalent interactions is observed when analyzing ![][image10]\-unsaturated degradation compounds, such as trans-2-heptenal, (E)-2-octenal, or (E,E)-2,4-decadienal. An exhaustive body of mass spectrometry and proteomics (2022-2023) dictates that these unsaturated aldehydes are subjected to Michael addition reactions.5

The conjugated carbon-carbon double bond in the ![][image10] position to the carbonyl group acts as an excellent acceptor for irreversible nucleophilic conjugate addition, being voraciously attacked by free sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of cysteine residues in SPI or PPI. The additional electron density and molecular rigidity imposed by ![][image14] hybridization limit the degree of conformational degrees of freedom (flexibility to rotate), promoting superior entrapment. Literature documents that unsaturated compounds with multiple double bonds (derived from linolenic acid) covalently anchor to SPI with much greater affinity and speed than saturated hexanal.44

### **6.3 Disulfide Exchange and the Collapse of Meaty Odorants (MFT)**

The critical link for meat formulation chemistry occurs with the retention of Maillard-derived thiols. It is assumed that the addition of exogenous ribose and cysteine in the extruder or homogenization tank will generate 2-methyl-3-furanthiol (MFT) and 2-furfurylthiol. Nevertheless, simultaneous instrumental quantifications repeatedly reflect a disconcerting collapse in free MFT concentrations present in the vapor phase of thermally treated SPI or PPI.20

The thiol of MFT (-SH) reacts strongly through a **disulfide interchange reaction**. By increasing the homogenization or extrusion temperature above the thermal denaturation temperature of globulin (typically ![][image15] \> 80-90 °C for SPI), the intact matrix rapidly unfolds, exposing internal cysteine amino acids. In this environment, the free \-SH groups of MFT directly attack endogenous disulfide bonds (-S-S-) or react oxidatively with exposed free sulfhydryl groups of the soy protein, creating a covalent polymeric bond.20

The analytical result is lapidary: the potent meat broth odorant remains macro-molecularly anchored to the physical structure of the soy or pea polymer. Consequently, MFT loses its vapor pressure and its absolute concentration (in ppb) in the headspace drops virtually to zero or well below the required olfactory threshold, annihilating the sought sensory contribution despite an initially successful synthesis in the reactor.20

### **6.4 The Final Competitive Cross-Talk: Lipid Aldehydes vs. Meaty Thiols**

The synthesis of quantitative data exposes the ultimate thermodynamic competition: the direct cross-talk between lipid pathways and covalent Maillard retention. The irreversible retention of MFT and 2-furfurylthiol is inexorably linked to coexisting concentrations of hexanal and unsaturated aldehydes.20

During the first minutes of thermal heating in intact SPI, rapid lipid thermal autoxidation infuses ![][image10]\-unsaturated aldehydes and diketones (such as diacetyl) into the medium. These compounds compete extremely aggressively for the same functional groups on the protein (lysine, and especially, free \-SH sulfhydryl groups via Michael addition). Quantitative investigations have established that the simultaneous presence of these lipid degradation molecules rapidly depletes the sulfhydryl inventory of the denatured protein matrix.20

Paradoxically, the initial unwanted abundance of lipid-derived aldehydes saturates the protein's covalent sites, momentarily decreasing the matrix's capacity to covalently anchor or trap the Maillard-synthesized 2-methyl-3-furanthiol. This dynamic creates extremely narrow kinetic time windows, where the abundance of MFT recovered in the headspace reaches an optimal transient peak due to the blocking of protein sites mediated by competing aldehydes.20 Eventually, as prolonged heating and oxygen supply continue, both Maillard compounds and lipid derivatives irrevocably sink into complex polymers, fostering "over-processed" aromatic profiles and a net drop in odor.10

## **7\. Simultaneous Quantification and Extrusion Dynamics: Matrix Integration**

The practical interrelation of all the mechanics previously exposed is exhibited in High Moisture Extrusion (HMPE). In the thermomechanical environment of a twin-screw extruder (where temperatures frequently reach 140-160 °C, pressures \>1 MPa, and high specific mechanical energy or SME), simultaneous interactions dictate the final olfactory composition.1

Residence time and enzymatic modification of protein pre-treatment define the final concentration of metabolites. GC-MS evaluations confirmed that HMPEs produced under moderate residence times (where hydrolysis and heating allowed optimal free amine release and reduced viscosity) minimized shear, significantly inhibiting lipid oxidation enzymes and drastically limiting absolute hexanal.10 However, if precursor supplementation and heating time exceeded a critical limit (\>40 min at reaction temperatures), 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine levels became overwhelming, providing chemical "burnt hair" notes despite successful underlying masking.10

## **8\. Concluding Remarks on the Rational Design of Protein-Flavor Interactions**

The systematic review of absolute quantitative approaches unequivocally demonstrates that intact Soy Protein Isolates (SPI) and Pea Protein Isolates (PPI) are fundamental reactants in the thermodynamic kinetics of aromas, and not simple inert carriers.

Successful flavor modulation in meat analogs requires an abandonment of empirical relative area (%) reports in favor of internal and isotopic standard calibrations (SIDA), as only the latter reveal the colossal stoichiometric discrepancies between baseline lipid aldehydes in the ![][image2]g/kg (ppm) range versus the sub-ppb demand for MFT and other potent thiol derivatives.1

The addition of Maillard precursors (e.g., cysteine and xylose/ribose) does not operate by isolating the meaty formative pathway. It triggers a polymeric antioxidant assault that directly neutralizes lipid free radicals, preventing the kinetic de novo appearance of hexanal.11 However, the exogenous sulfurous products that do manage to synthesize must survive a conformational minefield. The thermally unfolded quaternary structures of globulins will expose nucleophilic thiols that precipitate the disulfide exchange of 2-methyl-3-furanthiol and the covalent chemisorption of aldehydes via Michael addition and Schiff bases.20

Progress in this field, therefore, will mandatorily transition from heuristic formulation (trial and error) towards approaches of electrostatic and steric blocking or physical sequestration of the nucleophilic residues in protein matrices. Only by thermodynamically isolating the generated Maillard fractions, or manipulating the cross-competitiveness between lipid aldehydes and the disulfide network, will food science achieve a controlled transition toward stable organoleptic profiles distinguishable from mere perishable chemical simulation in plant-based proteins.
